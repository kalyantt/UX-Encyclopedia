Governance Is a Design Problem

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Governance Is a Design Problem. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Governance defines how recurring decisions are made, implemented, inspected, changed, and contested. It includes:

Governance is coordinated decision design

Host: What changes when we look at governance is coordinated decision design?

Guide: decision rights and accountable owners; principles, standards, policies, and reusable patterns; contribution and review routes; evidence and quality gates; versioning and communication; exceptions, escalation, and appeal; monitoring, incidents, and learning; authority to change, restrict, or retire the system.

Guide: Governance is not synonymous with central approval. Centralisation can improve consistency but create delay and detach decisions from local context. Full decentralisation can improve speed but duplicate work, fragment service promises, and distribute harm invisibly. Design the arrangement around consequence, recurrence, dependency, expertise, and reversibility.

Start from a cross-team failure

Host: What changes when we look at start from a cross-team failure?

Guide: Do not create a committee because mature organisations appear to have one. Identify the recurring coordination problem:

Guide: inconsistent interaction or content creates user confusion; accessibility or safety depends on specialist review too late; research evidence disappears between teams; shared data has unclear meaning or ownership; local changes break an end-to-end service promise; exceptions accumulate without changing the standard; incidents lack investigation or remediation authority.

Guide: Then create the smallest shared mechanism that can prevent, detect, or recover from it. A standard, owner, review trigger, contribution route, or common schema may solve the issue more effectively than another standing meeting.

Allocate decision rights explicitly

Host: What changes when we look at allocate decision rights explicitly?

Guide: For each recurring decision, name:

Guide: accountable owner: ultimately owns outcome and maintenance; decision-maker: has authority for this instance; required contributors: supply domain, user, technical, operational, accessibility, risk, or legal evidence; implementers: translate the decision into systems and service; people affected: must be represented or consulted where consequence warrants; reviewer or challenger: can independently question evidence and risk; escalation authority: resolves conflict or pauses action; informed roles: need the result and version.

Guide: Avoid consensus as the default. Collaboration supplies evidence and alternatives; decision rights prevent ambiguity. Equally, a named owner must not be allowed to ignore expertise or affected people without a traceable rationale.

Balance standards with local context

Host: What changes when we look at balance standards with local context?

Guide: Centralise when inconsistency creates serious user harm, interoperability depends on shared meaning, or specialist expertise is scarce. Decentralise when decisions are reversible, context varies legitimately, and teams can observe local outcomes. Use guardrails rather than prescriptions where multiple solutions can honour the same principle.

Guide: A design system demonstrates this balance. Components centralise accessibility, interaction behaviour, and maintenance; product teams choose composition for their context. Contributions and exceptions allow learning to return to the system. A rigid component forced into an unsuitable task is not governance success.

Make governance usable

Host: What changes when we look at make governance usable?

Guide: Governance itself has users, tasks, information architecture, feedback, and failure modes. People need to find the current rule, understand why it exists, know whether it applies, request review, contribute improvement, and receive a timely decision. Design:

Guide: one authoritative, versioned location; plain-language scope and examples; role-based routes and service levels; lightweight paths for low-risk decisions; transparent status and rationale; searchable decisions and exceptions; notifications for relevant changes; onboarding and support; accessible tools and alternate participation.

Guide: If compliance requires private networks and personal relationships, informal power replaces the stated process.

Learn from exceptions and friction

Host: What changes when we look at learn from exceptions and friction?

Guide: Track where teams bypass, misunderstand, or repeatedly challenge a rule. Non-adoption may mean weak communication, missing tools, delivery pressure, local need, or a flawed standard. Treat it as research rather than disobedience by default.

Guide: Review whether governance reduces repeated coordination cost, prevents incidents, shortens responsible decisions, improves reuse, and preserves outcome. Approval count is not value. A committee that reviews everything can create a new bottleneck while remaining unable to act after harm.

Transfer example: account recovery

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: account recovery?

Guide: If every product area designs recovery independently, identity evidence, delays, accessibility, support authority, and appeal diverge. Governance can define shared assurance levels, recovery states, content, audit, and escalation while allowing context-specific evidence.

Guide: Repeated exceptions from users without standard documents should change the shared model rather than remain local workarounds.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Bureaucracy substitution: process exists without a defined cross-team risk or value. Unclear authority: multiple groups advise, but nobody owns the decision or outcome. Approval at the end: governance reviews completed work after meaningful change is expensive. Rigid standard: consistency overrides task fit, inclusion, or local evidence.

Guide: Exception shadow: workarounds persist without visibility, expiry, or system learning. Committee latency: every decision follows the highest-friction route regardless of consequence. Document–system gap: written rules conflict with schemas, tools, metrics, or incentives. No remediation: governance can approve releases but cannot correct harm or compensate affected people.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior designers treat governance as service and organisational design. They decide what must be common, what may vary, who has power, how affected perspectives enter, and how evidence changes rules. They measure coordination quality rather than defending ceremony, and they redesign governance when its cost exceeds the risk it reduces.

Guide: They also recognise that standards distribute values. A shared default can scale access or scale exclusion. Governance must make those consequences inspectable and contestable.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a design-system component. Several teams need a consent dialog, but ownership of wording, accessibility, legal review, and future changes is unclear. Governance defines who can decide, test, release, and retire variants. Without those routes, even a well-crafted component becomes inconsistent or unsafe as it spreads.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Find an inconsistent pattern in a multi-product service—status language, consent, errors, navigation, or recovery. Trace who currently owns each variation and which affected person pays the coordination cost. Locate the official standard and try to request an exception.

Guide: If the route or authority is unclear, the inconsistency is organisationally designed even if nobody intended it.

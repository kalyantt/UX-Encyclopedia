Testing Assumptions, Not Ideas

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Testing Assumptions, Not Ideas. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Ideas are bundles of assumptions. Test the assumption with the highest risk first: that people want the outcome, can understand the solution, will use it in context, that it can be delivered, or that it will create value without unacceptable harm. This makes learning faster and protects teams from polishing an unproven premise.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Write a testable hypothesis with a predicted behaviour and a threshold for changing direction. Match fidelity to the question: a sketch can test comprehension; a concierge service can test demand; a technical spike can test feasibility. Do not build a full product merely to learn a narrow fact.

Test the weakest link with the smallest honest representation

Host: What changes when we look at test the weakest link with the smallest honest representation?

Guide: Tests match the claim. A data audit tests coverage and freshness; a comprehension prototype tests interpretation; a concierge workflow tests ownership; a limited pilot tests behaviour under real conditions. Each test defines expected evidence, threshold, limitation, and resulting decision before it runs. A positive reaction cannot compensate for a failed safety assumption.

Worked contrast: a successful test can answer the wrong question

Host: What changes when we look at worked contrast: a successful test can answer the wrong question?

Guide: Six participants correctly use a polished clinic-comparison prototype. The team concludes that comparison is validated. In reality, the prototype contained accurate hand-authored options, while the production risk is whether supplier data can identify clinically equivalent, current availability. The usability assumption gained support; feasibility and safety remain untested.

Decompose the bet

Host: What changes when we look at decompose the bet?

Guide: List assumptions across desirability, usability, viability, feasibility, accessibility, ethics, data, operations, and adoption. Phrase each so evidence could prove it weaker: “Target customers can recognise an acceptable substitute before checkout,” not “people like substitutions.”

Guide: Rate uncertainty and consequence if false. Test the riskiest assumption with the smallest method that can change the decision: interview or observation, fake door, prototype, concierge, technical spike, policy review, or controlled rollout. One test rarely validates the whole idea.

Guide: Define evidence and decision thresholds in advance. Record result, limits, and next action. Positive reactions do not prove behaviour; successful use does not prove value; technical feasibility does not prove responsible deployment. Assumption testing keeps these confidence layers separate.

Design the smallest test that can change the decision

Host: What changes when we look at design the smallest test that can change the decision?

Guide: The test plan states predicted evidence and possible actions before results arrive. Positive reaction cannot compensate for failed safety or feasibility. A fake door may estimate interest but cannot promise a care route that does not exist. Manual delivery can reveal value but not prove automation or scale.

Guide: Results update the assumption register and next decision, including stop or narrow. The team values invalidated ideas because they prevent expensive commitment; it does not redesign the metric until the idea appears successful.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: The riskiest assumption is often organisationally inconvenient rather than technically difficult. Name it anyway; otherwise a team can build certainty around the least important question.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Decompose an idea before testing it. A proposed “smart reminder” may assume the event can be predicted, people want a reminder, the timing is acceptable, the message is understandable, the delivery channel is trusted, and the reminder changes behaviour without causing stress or unwanted disclosure. Different assumptions need different evidence.

Guide: Testing the polish of the message cannot validate demand or feasibility.

Guide: Choose the least expensive representation that can answer the riskiest question. A story, interview, or observed workaround may test problem relevance; a sketch may test comprehension; a concierge or Wizard-of-Oz service may test whether people value a result; a technical prototype may test feasibility; a limited rollout may test real behaviour and unintended effects.

Guide: Define a decision rule before the study to reduce the temptation to reinterpret ambiguous results as approval.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Idea voting: preference for a polished concept substitutes for evidence. Wrong-fidelity test: a high-fidelity prototype is used to answer a simple demand question. Success without threshold: any positive comment is taken as a go signal. Ignored downside: a test observes benefit but not privacy, accessibility, support, or trust costs.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A fake-door test can estimate interest in same-day delivery but should not promise a consequential service without explaining availability. It cannot prove operational feasibility or willingness to pay.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Opportunity mapping identifies possible directions. Assumption testing decides what evidence is needed before investing. lesson 30 makes that learning visible to the people who must trust it.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Pick one solution and state its riskiest desirability, viability, feasibility, or usability assumption.

Conceptual Models

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Conceptual Models.

Guide: A conceptual model is the product’s designed explanation of what exists in the system, how those things relate, and what actions can change them. It is the bridge between the underlying technical system and the person’s mental model. Strong conceptual models make state, object relationships, and consequences predictable across the product.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Define the core objects, their attributes, their possible states, and the actions available to people. Then make these choices visible through language, navigation, controls, and feedback. A conceptual model is not an internal database schema; it is the useful explanation that lets people reason about the product.

Specify the designed world

Host: What changes when we look at specify the designed world?

Guide: Define the core objects, relationships, actions, states, rules, and vocabulary people must understand. Keep this model coherent across navigation, content, controls, data, and channels. A “project” cannot behave like a folder in one place and a permission group elsewhere without explanation.

Guide: Compare the conceptual model with people’s mental models and the implementation model. Expose distinctions that change action while hiding technical detail that does not. Use object and state diagrams, scenarios, and prototype prediction tasks to find contradictions before visual polish distributes them.

Test coherence, not recognition

Host: What changes when we look at test coherence, not recognition?

Guide: Apply the model across navigation, messages, service channels, and time. If the dashboard calls something a “case,” email calls it a “request,” and phone staff call it a “referral,” establish whether these are synonyms or different objects. If “cancel” is reversible in one place and final in another, the verb is not coherent.

Guide: Prototype tests should ask people to predict: If you change this slot, what remains? If the clinic asks for information, is the referral lost? Who can see this preparation note? What would you expect after cancelling? Errors reveal model contradictions earlier than preference questions do.

Define the model as objects, actions, relationships, and rules

Host: What changes when we look at define the model as objects, actions, relationships, and rules?

Guide: The team tests it through scenarios: two offers, withdrawn request, changed clinic, delegated helper, stale availability, and uncertain delivery. Interface, content, service, data, and analytics must express the same distinctions. When teams cannot explain a state without exceptions, the model—not only the label—needs revision.

Make the product's concepts coherent across channels

Host: What changes when we look at make the product's concepts coherent across channels?

Guide: Simplicity comes from removing unnecessary concepts or making relationships intelligible, not from concealing consequential state. The team tests whether people can predict outcomes, explain current state, and recover from exceptions. A coherent conceptual model reduces the amount of interface explanation needed because every new encounter builds on the same underlying logic.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Inconsistency is often a model problem, not a component problem. Before standardising screens, examine whether teams disagree about what the product’s objects and states actually mean.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: A conceptual model is the product’s deliberate explanation of what exists, how things relate, what actions are possible, and what causes change. It gives an experience its internal logic. In an email product, for example, the model may include messages, threads, labels, folders, archives, drafts, recipients, and actions such as send, reply, delete, or restore.

Guide: When these objects and actions behave consistently, people can make useful predictions even before they know every feature.

Guide: Make the model explicit before producing individual screens. Define core objects, attributes, relationships, lifecycle states, permissions, and action consequences. Check it against users’ mental models and the service’s underlying rules.

Guide: A coherent model makes navigation, content, error messages, interaction patterns, and data structures easier to align; an incoherent one pushes complexity into labels, exceptions, and training.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Screen-first design: controls are designed before the product’s objects and relationships are understood. Inconsistent verbs: the same action word produces different outcomes in different places. Hidden state: people cannot tell what object they are acting on or what state it is in.

Guide: Metaphor leakage: a familiar metaphor is used beyond the point where the underlying system behaves like it.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: In a project tool, is a task a document, a conversation, a status, or an object that moves through a workflow? If the product cannot answer coherently, every new screen teaches a different rule.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Mental models belong to people; conceptual models belong to the design. lesson 16 examines one tool designers use to connect them: metaphor.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Describe how a product “thinks” in one sentence. Then test your claim against a different screen or workflow in the same product.

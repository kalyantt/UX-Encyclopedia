Organization and Labels

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Organization and Labels. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Organisation schemes group content by a shared characteristic: subject, task, audience, format, chronology, geography, or another useful dimension. Labels name those groups and actions. Both should use language people recognise, distinguish nearby choices, and remain consistent across the system.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Test labels in context, not in a design review. Words acquire meaning through their neighbours, position, audience, and consequence. Avoid internal jargon and ambiguous marketing language where people need to make a practical choice.

Select a scheme, then test its consequences

Host: What changes when we look at select a scheme, then test its consequences?

Guide: Organisation schemes can be exact—alphabet, geography, time, identifier—or ambiguous—topic, task, audience, metaphor, and hybrid. Exact schemes are predictable when people know the value they need; ambiguous schemes support exploration but require interpretation. Most products combine them.

Guide: The design question is not which scheme is universally best, but which distinction people can recognise at the moment of choice.

Guide: Testing separates placement from wording. Open and closed card sorts reveal grouping tendencies, but they do not prove navigation. Tree testing measures first choice, path, backtracking, and confidence for representative tasks. First-click and comprehension tests reveal whether labels create the right expectation.

Guide: The team pays special attention to confidently wrong choices, which indicate strong but misleading scent.

Transfer example: household banking

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: household banking?

Guide: Customers may think in goals—pay, save, borrow—while a bank thinks in products and legal entities. A hybrid architecture can use task entry points while preserving account type as a precise object. Labelling a credit-card balance as “money you owe” may aid comprehension, but legal and statement contexts still require the authoritative product name.

Choose schemes by retrieval need

Host: What changes when we look at choose schemes by retrieval need?

Guide: Exact schemes such as alphabet, chronology, geography, and number are predictable when the property is known. Ambiguous schemes such as topic, task, audience, or metaphor depend on interpretation. Combine schemes when people approach the same content with different knowledge and goals, but keep each local grouping coherent.

Guide: Labels should use audience language, distinguish siblings, predict destination, and remain consistent with content. Test them out of context and in navigation. Card sorting can reveal grouping models; tree testing evaluates whether the proposed hierarchy supports retrieval. Search terms and support language expose synonyms and missing concepts.

Guide: Govern labels and categories as content changes. A label that once described a small collection can become misleading after growth. Avoid organisational jargon and buckets such as “Other” that hide unresolved structure.

Design labels as promises

Host: What changes when we look at design labels as promises?

Guide: He separates organisation schemes that are often mixed accidentally: by topic, task, audience, lifecycle stage, location, or organisational owner. A clinic directory organised by specialty helps someone who knows the clinical term but not someone following a referral. A task-based route helps immediate action but may hide long-term records.

Guide: The solution can use a stable primary scheme with facets or cross-links rather than forcing one hierarchy to satisfy every strategy.

Guide: The label inventory includes source term, proposed user-facing term, definition, synonyms, prohibited ambiguities, owner, and evidence. When service policy changes, the vocabulary changes deliberately across channels. This turns naming from last-minute copy polish into architecture and risk management.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Taxonomy is governance. New labels, categories, and content owners need decision rules or the structure decays as the organisation grows.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Choose an organisation scheme according to how people seek information. Exact schemes—alphabetical, chronological, geographical—work when the person knows the item’s attribute. Ambiguous schemes—topic, audience, task, metaphor—require interpretation but may better match a person’s goal.

Guide: Hybrid systems are common: a service might offer task-led navigation, audience-specific guidance, and a searchable topic index. Make the scheme visible through consistent grouping and predictable label grammar.

Guide: Labels must create a distinction at the moment of choice. Test them beside their neighbours, with realistic content and consequences. A word that works in a workshop can fail when it competes with five similar terms or means different things to different roles. Use controlled vocabularies and synonym management where a system is large enough to need them.

Guide: Align navigation labels, page headings, search terms, metadata, and support language so the system does not teach several names for the same thing without reason.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Ambiguous siblings: two labels appear different but people cannot predict the distinction. Marketing over meaning: expressive names obscure practical destinations. Vocabulary drift: navigation, search, and content use conflicting terms for the same concept. Rigid taxonomy: categories ignore the multiple ways people actually seek information.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: “Resources,” “Solutions,” and “Discover” may sound polished, but they make a person guess. A label succeeds when it helps the intended audience predict what is behind it.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: IA defines the structure; labels make it legible. lesson 39 shows how navigation turns that structure into orientation and movement.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Find a label that only an insider would understand. Rewrite it for someone trying to complete a real task.

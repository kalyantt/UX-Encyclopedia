Writing for Stress and Error

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Writing for Stress and Error.

Guide: Error content is interaction design under pressure. A useful message identifies the problem in plain language, preserves context, states the consequence, offers a concrete next step, and gives a path to help when recovery is not possible. Tone should be humane without becoming vague, jokey, or blaming.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Write for the person’s likely state: rushed, worried, unfamiliar, or interrupted. Do not hide material information behind generic reassurance. A good message reduces uncertainty; it does not merely acknowledge that the system has failed.

Write for reduced attention and urgent judgment

Host: What changes when we look at write for reduced attention and urgent judgment?

Guide: Stress narrows attention, weakens working memory, and makes ambiguity more costly. Error content should help the person recognise what happened, understand what state is safe, choose a next action, and preserve dignity. It should not demand that they decode a system code, remember previous entries, or infer whether retrying will create harm.

Guide: An effective error pattern includes a human description, the affected object or action, current known state, what has and has not happened, one primary recovery action, alternatives where necessary, and diagnostic detail for support. The tone is calm and accountable.

Guide: It avoids blame (“you entered an invalid value”), vague apology, false reassurance, and jokes in consequential moments. Technical codes can remain available for staff without leading the customer message.

Guide: The team rehearses errors under realistic conditions: interrupted connection, expired session, missing permission, stale availability, assistive technology, long translations, and repeated failure. It measures recovery completion, duplicate action, abandonment, support transfer quality, and whether people can accurately explain the state.

Guide: A message that feels friendly but produces the wrong next action has failed.

Transfer example: payment uncertainty

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: payment uncertainty?

Guide: After a checkout timeout, “Payment failed—try again” may charge the customer twice. The product should verify transaction state, preserve the basket, show what is known, and delay the retry action until it is safe. Writing for error begins with system truth and recovery logic.

Write for reduced capacity

Host: What changes when we look at write for reduced capacity?

Guide: Stress narrows attention and working memory. Lead with what happened, what it means, and the next safe action. Use short familiar sentences, specific labels, and information hierarchy; remove blame, humour, codes, and reassurance that conceals uncertainty.

Guide: An error message is part of a recovery system. Identify the affected object and field, preserve input, distinguish user-correctable from service failure, and provide an action the system can fulfil. For consequential states, explain whether money, data, or submission changed and how to reach authoritative help.

Guide: Test with realistic failure, time pressure, assistive technology, and support handoff. Track repeat attempts, abandonment, contact reason, and resolution. Rewriting cannot compensate for a dead end; sometimes the content finding is evidence that policy or system behaviour must change.

Write for diminished capacity without removing agency

Host: What changes when we look at write for diminished capacity without removing agency?

Guide: The team tests messages under realistic pressure, interruption, zoom, screen-reader navigation, and low connectivity. They observe whether people can explain the state and choose a safe next step—not whether they call the copy “friendly.”

Guide: Error content is monitored after release through repeated attempts, abandonment, support contacts, and unresolved cases, with owners for both the words and the underlying failure.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Error messages often expose a deeper service problem. Pair content fixes with better state modelling, support policy, and observability instead of asking language to compensate for a broken system.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Under stress, people scan for the immediate question: What happened? Is anything lost or at risk? What should I do now? Structure content in that order. State the specific problem without technical blame, explain the impact if material, give a concrete next action, and describe available help or timing.

Guide: Keep the error anchored to the relevant object or field so a person does not have to search for the problem while anxious.

Guide: Different failures need different language. A validation error may need a correction; a temporary outage needs status and reassurance about preserved work; a policy refusal needs an honest explanation and recourse; a high-risk security event needs a safe, unambiguous action. Avoid artificial cheerfulness, euphemism, or humour when it makes consequence unclear.

Guide: Coordinate messages with the actual support, policy, and recovery paths behind them.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Generic apology: the product acknowledges failure but gives no diagnosis or next step. Blaming language: the person is told they “did it wrong” when the system may be at fault. False reassurance: wording suggests success or safety before the service can guarantee it. Unsupported escalation: “contact support” is offered where support cannot resolve the issue.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: “Something went wrong” tells a person nothing when a payment fails. They need to know what happened, what it means, whether their money moved, and what to do next.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Content makes the normal flow understandable. Error content protects the experience when the normal flow breaks. Module 8 moves from content and IA to the interaction and visual craft of those moments.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Collect three error messages and rank them by usefulness under stress. Which gives a person the clearest recovery path?

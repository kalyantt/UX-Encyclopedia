UX Encyclopedia · Two-voice episode

What UX is

Alex and a guide work through the same concepts and CarePath case taught in UX-001. This is an audio adaptation, not a replacement for the written lesson and its challenges.

The message

Guide: Maya looks at her phone. Request received. She puts it away. Then she picks it up again. Alex, does she have an appointment?

Alex: I think so? Wait. No. The screen doesn't actually say that.

Guide: Right. Her referral seemed to disappear between two services days ago. Now she has another message she can't act on.

Alex: Oh... I was about to polish that confirmation screen. I hadn't followed what happened before it.

Guide: The copy matters. But start with Maya's attempt. What did she come here to do?

Alex: Get care. Without chasing the system or retelling her story at every step.

Guide: Yes. That's a much bigger design brief than making a confirmation screen look reassuring.

Alex: So when we say user experience, we're talking about the whole attempt, not one screen?

Guide: Exactly. What the person perceives and feels while using a product over time. Did it help them do the thing they came to do? What did it cost them?

Alex: A lovely checkout can fail if it hides the delivery date. A plain government form can succeed if it gets someone through a stressful task with certainty.

Guide: That's it. The interface is part of the experience. It isn't the entire experience.

Alex: Then where does interaction design fit? Is that the buttons, forms, and cards?

Guide: It's the design of interactive products that support how people work, communicate, and live. Choices and feedback are central. But so are the circumstances around them.

Alex: Which means research, accessibility, psychology, information architecture, engineering, privacy, service design...

Guide: And product decisions, ergonomics, ethics, even AI when it shapes the result. No single specialist owns Maya's experience.

Alex: Before I draw anything, I should ask what Maya knows, what she needs to achieve, what a safe outcome looks like, and what failure would cost her.

Follow the experience

Guide: Good. Let's trace one attempt. First, a person has a goal. Then they take an action. The system responds. An outcome follows. The person makes meaning from it.

Alex: Hang on. Why separate the response from the outcome?

Guide: Because the system can say, Request received, while the person's actual goal remains unresolved.

Alex: Oh. The response is what the interface says happened. The outcome is whether she really made progress.

Guide: Yes. Then comes the question designers often skip: what does Maya believe it means? Does she feel confident, safe, annoyed, or fooled? Does she continue or give up?

Alex: So at the goal I ask, what am I trying to do? At the action, can I tell what to do? At the response, did the system explain itself?

Guide: And at the outcome: did I make progress? At the meaning: can I trust that progress? Then what happens next?

Alex: That makes the same confirmation look completely different. Technically correct, emotionally useless.

Guide: Sometimes. Now zoom underneath that loop. There's purpose and user need: is this helping a real activity?

Alex: Then interaction: are choices and feedback understandable?

Guide: Then information and interface: words, structure, controls, and timing. Then system and service: data, policy, people, logistics, and technology.

Alex: And finally the consequence. Does Maya leave relieved, competent, uncertain, frustrated, or harmed?

Guide: Exactly. Five layers. A product can succeed at one and fail at another.

Alex: Like a coffee app whose map pin is easy to tap but sends my order to the wrong branch.

Guide: The control worked. The experience didn't. Or a bank transfer completes safely, but the confirmation is unclear, so you're afraid to close the app.

Alex: Oh! We don't get to declare victory because the UI did what we programmed it to do.

Guide: Right. But the whole-system view doesn't mean redesigning everything at once. It helps you find where the failure actually lives.

Away from the screen

Alex: Can we try another setting? Suppose I check in for a flight on my phone.

Guide: You're tired, carrying luggage, and your connection keeps dropping. What would a beautiful form fail to solve?

Alex: I need a clear next step, document requirements I understand, and progress that survives an interruption.

Guide: And durable proof that check-in succeeded. What else touches the experience?

Alex: The email, the kiosk, baggage rules, and the staff handoff. The app is one touchpoint.

Guide: Now write an experience hypothesis. For this person, in this situation, we help them make progress on a goal by supporting an activity, without a serious cost or risk.

Alex: And name the service conditions needed to make that promise true. That's more demanding than, make checkout better.

Guide: Exactly. If delivery capacity is unreliable, a tracking animation won't make delivery reliable.

Alex: It could communicate uncertainty better. But that is a different claim.

Maya's whole journey

Guide: Now back to CarePath. The team was asked to improve appointment booking. Sounds specific. What's hidden in that request?

Alex: It assumes booking is the problem. And that another interface is the answer.

Guide: So follow Maya. She has recurring migraines. She wants appropriate help without losing a workday or repeating her medical story.

Alex: First she searches for the right clinic. The website uses service names she doesn't recognize.

Guide: She calls reception and waits. She learns she needs a referral. Her doctor sends one. Can Maya see whether it arrived?

Alex: No. Days pass. Then a text finally gives her a booking link, but the slot clashes with work.

Guide: And the rescheduling rule is unclear. She submits a request. The screen says, Request received.

Alex: That's the moment we started with. She isn't asking whether the request was received. She's asking whether she can count on an appointment.

Guide: Exactly. On the day, the clinic asks her to re-enter information she already supplied online. Afterward, a payment message arrives, but no clear follow-up plan.

Alex: If I only inspect booking, I miss search language, referral policy, staff capacity, work constraints, duplicate data, payment, and follow-up.

Guide: Some are interface choices. Others are rules, human work, or data movement. Maya experiences their combined result.

Alex: Then I need to rewrite the brief before I draw the fix.

Guide: Go on. What should CarePath help her do?

Alex: Reach appropriate non-emergency care while managing work, health, and uncertainty. Know what's required, preserve her information, and understand what happens next.

Guide: And what must it avoid?

Alex: Avoidable repetition. Hidden status. Unsafe delay.

Guide: That's an experience statement, not a prescribed solution. Now the team can ask harder questions. What counts as appropriate care? Which delays are necessary?

Alex: What information can be reused safely? Where is human judgment essential? Who can't use a digital route?

Guide: And if referral and booking systems disagree, who owns recovery?

Alex: The first artifact is an experience brief: starting condition, desired outcome, actors, touchpoints, constraints, serious failure consequences, and unknowns.

Guide: Exactly. Navigation should help someone find care. Content should reduce uncertainty. A metric should show meaningful progress, not just form completion.

Alex: And we shouldn't add an AI feature if it creates ambiguity or an impression of authority without improving the care journey.

Guide: See what happened? The project changed before a screen changed. Booking became one part of an attempt to reach care.

Alex: The brief is version one, though. We'd still need evidence about different people, contexts, risks, and what good outcomes mean.

Three ways to zoom

Guide: There's a senior-level judgment here. At what resolution do you study and fix a problem? Try three: moment, journey, system.

Alex: At the moment: Maya picks a slot. Can she find the action, understand the feedback, and recover from a mistake?

Guide: Good. At the journey?

Alex: Connect discovery, referral, booking, preparation, attendance, payment, and follow-up. Does information survive each handoff? Can she resume after interruption?

Guide: And at the system?

Alex: Capacity, staff roles, data standards, identity, consent, clinical rules, vendor systems, accessibility, governance, and incentives.

Guide: Exactly. A reminder cannot create an appointment when there is no capacity. A clearer error cannot repair a referral that nobody owns.

Alex: So I can fix the misleading message without pretending I've fixed healthcare. But I also have to surface the service failure.

Guide: Yes. Find the owner. A small copy fix may be right. So may a service change. Don't mistake the visible surface for the experienced problem.

What changes now

Alex: Let me try the traps. Treat UX as a polish phase. Count a click as success even if Maya never reaches care. Chase delight when she needs certainty.

Guide: And?

Alex: Design only the happy path. Ignore waiting, errors, handoffs, and support.

Guide: That's it. When the team debates a screen, bring it back to her goal and the cost of failure. Connect local craft to system consequence.

Alex: Maya read, Request received. But the question she was really asking was, Can I trust what happens next?

Guide: Listen for that in your own products. After a payment, a save message, a loading state, or an error: what does the response make you believe happened?

Alex: And what evidence do I have?

Guide: Exactly. Pick one task you completed today. Trace your goal, first action, system response, actual outcome, and the feeling you carried away.

Alex: Then find the moment where design changed that feeling. I may never look at a confirmation message the same way again.

Guide: Good. Next lesson, we'll test this against usability. Later come emotion, context, service, and trade-offs. For now, remember the gap between a successful interaction and a successful experience.

A conversational adaptation of UX-001. Maya and CarePath are fictional teaching examples. The written lesson and challenges remain the source of truth.

UX Encyclopedia · Two-voice episode

What UX is

Alex and a guide work through the same concepts and CarePath case taught in UX-001. This is an audio adaptation, not a replacement for the written lesson and its challenges.

The moment

Guide: Maya looks at her phone. The screen says, Request received. She puts it away. Then she picks it up again. Does she have an appointment? Or has she only joined a queue?

Alex: I was looking at that confirmation screen. The copy feels vague. Maybe I should rewrite it.

Guide: Maybe. But follow Maya backwards for a moment. Her referral seemed to disappear between two services days ago. She has been unsure what is happening ever since.

Alex: Oh. So the sentence on the screen is part of the problem, but it isn't the whole problem.

Guide: Exactly. UX is not a layer of polish painted onto an interface. It is what someone perceives and feels while using a product over time, including whether it helps them do the thing they came to do.

Alex: Then the unit of analysis isn't the screen. It's the person's attempt.

Guide: Yes. What were they trying to do? What happened? What did it cost them? And how did they feel afterward? A beautiful checkout can be poor UX if it hides the delivery date. A plain public-service form can be excellent UX if it helps someone finish a stressful task with certainty.

Alex: Where does interaction design fit? Is it just the buttons and cards?

Guide: It designs interactive products that support how people work, communicate, and live. That includes choices and feedback, but not just controls. Psychology, ergonomics, visual and product design, engineering, information architecture, research, accessibility, privacy, ethics, and AI can all shape the result. No one specialist owns the experience.

Alex: So before I draw anything, I should ask what Maya is actually doing. What she knows. What a safe outcome looks like. What could fail.

Guide: And what must be true when she finishes. Those questions keep a team from polishing one moment while missing the job.

The mechanism

Guide: Here is a way to follow an experience in motion. A person has a goal. They take an action. The system responds. There is an outcome. The person interprets it. Then they decide what to do next.

Alex: Can we make that less abstract?

Guide: At the goal, ask: What am I here to accomplish? At the action: Can I tell what to do? At the response: Did the system explain what happened? At the outcome: Did I make progress? At the interpretation: Do I feel confident, annoyed, safe, or fooled? And then: Would I continue, or abandon this?

Alex: That last part changes it. The same confirmation could say the request went through, but leave Maya too unsure to continue.

Guide: Right. People judge results and meaning, not pixels in isolation. Now look at the five layers underneath that loop. First, purpose and user need: is the product helping with a real activity? Second, interaction: can the person choose and understand the feedback? Third, information and interface: are the words, structure, controls, and timing useful? Fourth, system and service: do the data, policies, people, logistics, and technology deliver what was promised? Fifth, experience and consequence: does the person leave with relief, competence, uncertainty, trust, frustration, or harm?

Alex: An interface can succeed at its own layer while the experience fails at another.

Guide: Precisely. Imagine a coffee app with an easy-to-tap map pin that sends the order to the wrong branch. The control worked. The experience did not. Or a secure bank transfer that completes correctly, then shows an unclear confirmation. The system is right, but the person is afraid to close the app.

Alex: Oh. We don't get to declare success just because the UI did what we programmed it to do.

Guide: That's the whole-system view. Not a demand to redesign everything at once. A reminder to find the layer where the failure actually lives.

Another situation

Alex: What if we leave healthcare for a minute? Say I check in for a flight on my phone.

Guide: You're tired. Your connection is unreliable. You're carrying luggage. You may be worried about missing the flight. Better-looking form fields alone won't rescue you. You need a clear next step, progress that survives interruption, understandable document requirements, and durable proof that check-in succeeded.

Alex: And the app is only one touchpoint. The email, kiosk, baggage rules, and staff handoff can change what that success means.

Guide: Exactly. Before proposing a solution, write an experience hypothesis. For a person in a particular situation, we will help them make progress on a goal by supporting an activity, so they achieve an outcome without a serious cost or risk. Then list the service and organisational conditions that must be true for that promise to hold.

Alex: More demanding than, Make checkout better. It forces me to name the person, the activity, the outcome, and the constraints.

Guide: It also shows you when a screen cannot fix the problem. If delivery capacity is unreliable, a new tracking animation won't make delivery reliable. It might communicate uncertainty better. Those are different claims.

CarePath

Guide: Now return to Maya. Alex's team has been asked to improve appointment booking. It sounds specific. But it assumes booking is the problem and an interface is the answer.

Alex: So I follow her attempt to reach care before opening a design file.

Guide: Maya has recurring migraines. Her goal is appropriate help, without losing a day of work or telling the same medical story again and again. First she tries to find the right clinic. The website names services in language she doesn't recognise.

Alex: She calls reception, waits, and learns that she needs a referral.

Guide: Her doctor sends one. Maya cannot see whether it arrived. Days pass. A text finally brings a booking link, but the available slot clashes with work. The rescheduling rule is unclear. Then that message appears: Request received.

Alex: She's not asking whether the request was received. She's asking whether she can count on an appointment.

Guide: On the day, the clinic asks her to re-enter information she already supplied online. After the visit, a payment message arrives, but no clear follow-up plan.

Alex: If I study only the booking form, I miss the search words, referral policy, staff capacity, timing, work constraints, duplicated data, payment, and follow-up.

Guide: Some are interface choices. Others are rules, human work, data movement, or organisational boundaries. Maya experiences their combined result. That's why Alex rewrites the assignment before trying to solve it.

Alex: For a person seeking non-emergency care while managing work, health, and uncertainty, CarePath should help them reach an appropriate appointment, understand what is required, preserve their information, and know what happens next, without avoidable repetition, hidden status, or unsafe delay.

Guide: That is an experience statement, not a solution. It gives the team a unit of analysis. Now it can ask sharper questions: What counts as appropriate care? Which delays are necessary, and which are operational waste? What information can be reused safely? What needs human judgement? Who cannot use a digital route? Who owns recovery if referral and booking systems disagree?

Alex: The first artifact is an experience brief. I record the starting condition, desired outcome, actors, touchpoints, constraints, serious failure consequences, and unknowns.

Guide: And every later artifact must connect to it. Navigation should help someone find the right service. Content should reduce uncertainty. A metric should show meaningful progress, not just form completion. An AI feature should be rejected if it adds ambiguity or apparent authority without helping the care journey.

Alex: So the project changed before a single screen changed. Booking became one episode inside a longer attempt to reach care. We will evaluate that attempt, not optimise booking conversion in isolation. The brief is version one, and we still need evidence about different people, contexts, risks, and good outcomes.

Three resolutions

Guide: There is a senior-level skill hidden here: choosing the right resolution for a decision without losing the connections. Look at the moment, the journey, and the system.

Alex: At the moment level, Maya selects a slot and sees a response. Can she find the action? Is feedback timely? Does it tell her what happened? Can she recover if she made a mistake?

Guide: At the journey level, connect discovery, referral, booking, preparation, attendance, payment, and follow-up. Does information survive the handoffs? Do policies contradict each other? Can Maya resume after an interruption? Does each step prepare her for the next?

Alex: And at the system level: clinic capacity, staff roles, data standards, identity, consent, clinical rules, vendor systems, accessibility, governance, and incentives.

Guide: Yes. A reminder cannot create an appointment where there is no capacity. A clearer error cannot repair a referral nobody owns. Interface fixes still matter; they just have to be honest about the layer they can change. Ask what happens in the moment, what it does to the journey, and what system condition produces it. Sometimes the responsible move is a small copy fix. Sometimes the interface reveals a service failure that needs another owner. The point is not to make every project enormous. It is to avoid mistaking the visible surface for the experienced problem.

The harder decision

Alex: Let me try the mistakes I might make. I could treat UX as the visual-polish phase after the real product work. I could count a click as success even if the person never gets the outcome. I could chase delight when Maya needs speed, certainty, and recovery. Or I could design the happy path and forget waiting, error states, handoffs, and support.

Guide: Exactly. When a team debates a screen, bring it back to the person's goal and the cost of failure. You can fix a misleading confirmation without pretending you have redesigned healthcare. At the same time, document the referral-state or ownership problem the interface cannot solve. Senior design connects local craft to system consequence, and makes sure someone owns the outcome across boundaries.

Alex: Maya read, Request received. The real question was, Can I trust what happens next?

Guide: That's the question to take into your own products. After a payment, a save message, a loading state, or an error, pause. What does the response make you believe happened? What evidence does it give you? Then pick one task you completed today and trace five things: your goal, your first action, the response, the outcome, and the feeling you carried away. Find the moment where design changed that feeling.

Alex: Next time, I'll stop asking only whether a screen looks good. I'll ask whether the whole attempt helped someone make progress.

Guide: In the next lesson, we'll put that idea under pressure by looking at usability. Later, we'll add emotion, context, service, and trade-offs. For now, listen for the gap between a successful interaction and a successful experience.

Predictions and Anomalies Need Uncertainty and Next Actions

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Predictions and Anomalies Need Uncertainty and Next Actions.

Guide: A prediction estimates an unknown or future outcome under defined conditions. An anomaly signal indicates that a case differs from a reference pattern. Neither specifies what should happen next. Product design connects the signal to a person, choice, authority, time horizon, error trade-off, and available intervention.

A prediction is evidence, not a decision

Host: What changes when we look at a prediction is evidence, not a decision?

Guide: Write the decision before designing the score:

Guide: Who decides what, and by when? What outcome is predicted, over what period, and relative to which population? What alternative actions are available? Can acting now change the outcome? What evidence besides the score should inform judgement? What happens after a false positive, false negative, true positive, and true negative? Who bears each cost, and who can obtain recourse?

Guide: If no useful action exists, surfacing a prediction may add anxiety or workload without value. The team may need to create an intervention before creating an alert.

Thresholds encode value and capacity

Host: What changes when we look at thresholds encode value and capacity?

Guide: A continuous score becomes operational only when thresholds map ranges to behaviour: no action, monitor, request information, review, intervene, or stop. Moving a threshold changes false positives, false negatives, workload, and which people receive attention.

Guide: Thresholds should reflect:

Guide: severity and reversibility of each error; prevalence of the outcome; quality and timeliness of available intervention; review capacity and response time; differences across relevant populations and contexts; cost of interruption and alert fatigue; the system’s calibration and coverage; fairness and policy constraints.

Guide: Do not optimise sensitivity while pretending review capacity is infinite. A queue that exceeds staff capacity changes the product: alerts arrive too late, reviewers skim, and unflagged work receives less attention. Capacity is part of the threshold model.

Make uncertainty actionable

Host: What changes when we look at make uncertainty actionable?

Guide: “High risk” lacks the reference class, outcome, time horizon, and evidence boundary needed for judgement. Good presentation answers:

Guide: risk of what event; during which period; compared with what baseline; what information contributed and which is missing or stale; whether the score is calibrated for this context; what changed since the last review; what action is permitted and likely to help; when to escalate or disregard the signal.

Guide: Avoid treating feature importance as causal explanation. “Long travel time contributed to this score” does not prove that shortening travel will prevent non-attendance; it may be correlated with another condition. Describe factors as signals unless causal evidence supports an intervention.

Guide: A numeric probability is useful when trained users understand its calibration and decision threshold. For many people, an action band with evidence and uncertainty is more usable: “Check transport and appointment time within 48 hours; contact preference missing.” Do not hide the actual probability from expert inspection if it supports accountable review.

Design an alert system, not an alert card

Host: What changes when we look at design an alert system, not an alert card?

Guide: Alerts compete for scarce attention. Design priority, grouping, suppression, expiry, escalation, ownership, history, and resolution. Show what is new since the last view. Preserve why an alert was closed; dismissal, disagreement, resolved barrier, duplicate, and expired opportunity are different outcomes.

Guide: Measure the joint system: detection, appropriate intervention, time to action, queue age, workload, override and disagreement, repeat alerts, missed cases, person-level outcome, and group-level distribution. Review whether performance changes as staff learn to rely on or game the score.

Feedback is changed by the prediction

Host: What changes when we look at feedback is changed by the prediction?

Guide: Record prediction, action, exposure, response, and outcome separately. Do not use a coordinator’s hurried dismissal as ground truth. Where possible, evaluate interventions causally rather than assuming correlation proves benefit.

Transfer example: payment anomaly

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: payment anomaly?

Guide: A bank detects a transaction different from a customer’s usual pattern. “Fraud detected” converts anomaly into conclusion. A better design states the unusual attributes, asks the customer to confirm, enables an immediate protective action, and provides human review. Thresholds reflect theft risk, customer interruption, transaction value, channel, and review capacity.

Guide: The anomaly supports a decision; it does not become the decision.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Score-as-command: colour and ranking turn probability into unquestioned instruction. Undefined reference: “high” omits outcome, horizon, baseline, and population. No effective action: the alert produces anxiety or a queue without a viable intervention. Capacity blindness: thresholds generate more review than the service can perform.

Guide: Correlation as cause: a contributing feature is presented as the lever that will change the outcome. Stigmatising label: system or service barriers become a judgement about the person. Feedback contamination: intervention-altered outcomes are treated as untouched ground truth. Quiet allocation harm: unflagged cases lose human attention because the score reorganises work.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior practitioners recognise that a threshold is a policy choice allocating attention, error, and opportunity. They bring affected people, domain experts, operations, and accountable leaders into that decision; publish prohibited uses; ensure intervention capacity; and monitor both who receives help and who becomes invisible.

Guide: They demand evidence that acting on the prediction improves the intended outcome. Better discrimination without an effective, ethical intervention is not product success.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a maintenance dashboard. A model flags a machine as likely to fail. The alert should convey uncertainty, time horizon, relevant evidence, and the next safe inspection step. A risk score without action can create alarm or automation bias; a false-negative path needs monitoring and escalation too.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Find a risk badge, recommendation score, forecast, or anomaly alert. Ask what event and period it describes, what baseline makes it high or low, what evidence is missing, and what action can change the outcome. Then look beyond the card: who owns it, how many alerts compete for attention, and what happens to people who are never flagged?

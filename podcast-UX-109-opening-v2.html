Requirements: User, Content, Data, Quality, and Constraint

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Requirements: User, Content, Data, Quality, and Constraint.

Guide: Requirements describe what a product or service must enable and the conditions it must satisfy. They can be user requirements, functional behaviours, content needs, data needs, quality attributes, operational constraints, accessibility commitments, legal obligations, or business boundaries. Good requirements retain a trace to evidence and a reason to exist.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Separate requirement from implementation. “A person can confirm or reschedule from the reminder” expresses a necessary capability. “Send an SMS with two buttons” is one possible implementation. This distinction preserves room to explore alternatives and makes review more rigorous.

Guide: Quality requirements deserve equal status. Reliability, privacy, accessibility, response time, recovery, and clarity are not polish added after core behaviour. They determine whether the nominal capability is safe and usable in reality.

Build a balanced requirement set

Host: What changes when we look at build a balanced requirement set?

Guide: User requirements describe outcomes and capabilities in context. Functional requirements describe system behaviour. Content requirements specify meaning, source, structure, ownership, and lifecycle. Data requirements cover fields, quality, lineage, permissions, retention, and states.

Guide: Quality attributes cover accessibility, performance, reliability, security, privacy, safety, and usability. Constraints identify policy, technology, budget, time, and operations.

Guide: Trace each to evidence and consequence. State measurable acceptance while avoiding false precision. “Respond within two seconds for 95% of eligible requests under defined load” is testable; “fast” is not. Include errors, absence, concurrency, translation, third parties, and recovery.

Guide: Resolve conflicts as product decisions. Stronger verification may affect access and time; richer data may affect privacy; consistency may conflict with expert flexibility. Record owner, rationale, residual risk, and validation method.

Guide: Maintain requirements with version and change impact. A requirement that cannot explain why it exists becomes difficult to challenge and easy to obey after the original need has disappeared.

Make quality measurable without pretending certainty

Host: What changes when we look at make quality measurable without pretending certainty?

Guide: Replace adjectives with conditions and evidence. “Fast” becomes a response distribution for a named operation, network profile, and percentile. “Accessible” points to the applicable standard and adds task-based testing with disabled participants. “Understandable” becomes accurate explanation and next-action selection in realistic scenarios.

Guide: Numbers should express a defensible threshold, not manufacture confidence.

Guide: Prioritisation must consider consequence and dependency, not only request volume. A rare duplicate-booking race may deserve attention because clinical capacity and patient trust are at stake. Record what is deferred, who bears the risk, and what signal triggers reconsideration.

Convert needs into testable conditions

Host: What changes when we look at convert needs into testable conditions?

Guide: Each requirement links to source evidence, owner, priority, acceptance method, and consequence if wrong. Conflicts—speed versus clinical review, personalisation versus privacy—are resolved explicitly rather than hidden in a backlog item. A clinical constraint may be fixed; a duplicated evidence request may only be inherited habit.

Write requirements as traceable agreements

Host: What changes when we look at write requirements as traceable agreements?

Guide: Content requirements specify message purpose, required meaning, hierarchy, variables, states, channel, and governance. Data requirements specify definition, source, freshness, validity, missingness, permissions, and lineage. Service requirements describe staff authority, handoff, capacity, escalation, and evidence.

Guide: Constraints are classified as law, safety, policy, contract, technology, capacity, or choice so they can be challenged appropriately.

Guide: Conflicts are resolved at the requirement level. Personalisation may conflict with minimisation; instant feedback may conflict with authoritative distributed state; a short form may conflict with necessary evidence. The decision record names the trade-off and guardrail. Requirements remain connected to scenarios, workflow, design, tests, and production measures.

Transfer example: delivery address

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: delivery address?

Guide: “Collect a valid address” is incomplete. The product needs country formats, building access, delivery instructions, privacy, autocomplete limitations, manual entry, validation source, and recovery when a technically valid address is operationally unreachable.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Trace each requirement to a source: user evidence, policy, operational reality, technical constraint, or strategic decision. Untraced requirements tend to become permanent assumptions.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: “Add appointment reminders” sounds like a requirement. It is a solution. The actual requirement may include a patient’s need to prepare, a clinic’s need to reduce no-shows, consent for reminders, a reliable contact method, accessible message content, a reschedule route, and a rule about when contact is appropriate.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Worked example For appointment reminders, user need is timely understandable notice and a viable change path. Content requires date, location, preparation, sender, and next action from authoritative sources. Data requires current contact preference and appointment state. Quality covers delivery, accessibility, privacy, and latency.

Guide: Policy defines when rescheduling is allowed; operations define capacity. A reminder screen alone satisfies only one slice of the requirement system. lesson 108 clarified the artifacts used to communicate intent. This lesson makes their content complete enough to guide design and delivery. lesson 110 offers the reusable decision tools that help apply those requirements.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Pick a feature statement. Rewrite it into one user, one quality, one data/content, and one constraint requirement—without naming a particular UI.

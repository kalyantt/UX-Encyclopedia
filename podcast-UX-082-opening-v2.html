AI Errors and Graceful Failure

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: AI Errors and Graceful Failure.

Guide: Probabilistic systems can be wrong without looking broken. They can produce a plausible fabrication, omit a decisive fact, apply a rule outside its context, retrieve stale information, misread ambiguous input, call the wrong tool, or execute a valid action with the wrong permission. People may not detect these failures because fluency and interface polish remain intact.

Design the failure, not only the ideal output

Host: What changes when we look at design the failure, not only the ideal output?

Guide: Graceful failure therefore has four jobs:

Guide: 1. Limit consequence: prevent or contain unsafe action. 2. Make state legible: distinguish known, inferred, missing, stale, and failed information. 3. Preserve progress: retain valid input, selections, and context. 4. Provide a changed route: clarify, verify, use an authoritative source, switch method, escalate, reverse, or appeal.

Guide: An apology is not recovery. “Try again” is not a changed route when the same boundary will persist.

Build a system-level failure taxonomy

Host: What changes when we look at build a system-level failure taxonomy?

Guide: Do not label every problem “the AI was wrong.” Locate where the experience can fail:

Guide: Input: ambiguous, incomplete, conflicting, out of scope, malicious, or inaccessible. Data and retrieval: absent, stale, duplicated, unauthorised, poorly ranked, or from the wrong source. Model: misclassification, hallucination, unstable generation, bias, or weak generalisation. Policy: unclear or conflicting rules; unsafe request accepted; safe request blocked.

Guide: Tool and integration: wrong API call, timeout, partial write, duplicate action, or stale state. Permission: data or action exceeds the person’s delegation contract. Interface interpretation: provenance, uncertainty, or role is misunderstood. Human workflow: reviewer overload, bad escalation, missing authority, or delayed response.

Guide: Downstream outcome: a technically valid output creates harm in the real service.

Guide: For each failure, record trigger, detection signal, affected people, consequence, reversibility, safe state, user message, preserved context, recovery route, escalation, owner, and learning path. Treat false positives and false negatives separately; their harms and safeguards often differ.

Abstention is a product capability

Host: What changes when we look at abstention is a product capability?

Guide: A mature system can decide that available evidence is insufficient for a dependable output. Abstention should be specific and useful:

Guide: say what cannot be established; identify the missing or conflicting evidence; request one answer that can genuinely resolve ambiguity; narrow the task to a part the system can support; present authoritative source material without inventing a conclusion; offer a human or deterministic route with expectations; preserve everything already entered.

Guide: Avoid treating low model confidence as the only trigger. A high-confidence output may still concern a prohibited action, stale source, unfamiliar population, or severe consequence. Conversely, uncertainty does not always require stopping; a low-risk draft can remain useful when clearly editable.

Preserve truth during partial action

Host: What changes when we look at preserve truth during partial action?

Guide: Agentic and integrated systems can fail midway. The interface must distinguish planned, started, completed, confirmed, reversed, and unresolved actions. Never infer success from a request being sent. Use idempotent operations where possible so retrying does not duplicate a booking or referral.

Guide: Show the person what changed, what did not, and what remains uncertain. A rollback may not erase a message already received or data already disclosed; describe residual effects honestly. Where reversal is impossible, provide remediation and accountable support rather than a decorative undo control.

Feedback and recourse serve different needs

Host: What changes when we look at feedback and recourse serve different needs?

Guide: Feedback helps improve a system. Recourse helps the affected person obtain correction or remedy now. Do not replace one with the other.

Guide: Useful feedback captures the relevant output, model/data version, source state, user correction, context, and consequence—subject to minimisation and permission. Let people add context without requiring technical diagnosis. Explain whether feedback affects their present case, future systems, or neither.

Guide: Recourse must reach a role with authority, have a response expectation, preserve evidence, and allow the outcome to change. High-severity incidents may require proactive notification, service recovery, regulatory processes, or support for people who never used the interface themselves.

Transfer example: AI writing assistant

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: ai writing assistant?

Guide: A writing tool cannot verify a cited statistic. Rather than fabricate a source or repeatedly regenerate, it should retain the draft, mark the unsupported sentence, offer to remove or rephrase the claim, and let the writer search an authoritative source.

Guide: The consequence is lower than a clinical referral, so human review is usually enough; the same recovery pattern is calibrated to a different risk.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Confident fabrication: uncertainty or missing evidence becomes fluent fact. Retry loop: repeated generation produces variation without a safer route. Context loss: failure removes input, comparison, or work needed to recover. False success: an initiated external action is presented as completed.

Guide: One generic fallback: every failure routes to the same apology or overloaded support queue. Feedback void: reports disappear without helping the affected person or responsible team. Recourse theatre: review exists but cannot change the outcome. User-as-debugger: people must classify technical errors or repeatedly reproduce sensitive cases.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior practitioners budget for failure as part of the product, service, and operating model. They define severity, incident ownership, response times, fallback capacity, evidence retention, affected-person communication, and post-incident learning before launch.

Guide: They test harmful but plausible outputs, compound failures, load, staff absence, vendor outages, and policy conflicts—not merely an amusing hallucination.

Guide: They also make failure visible upstream. If recurring recovery depends on heroic human intervention, the feature is externalising its true cost onto operations and users.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a route-planning assistant. When live transit data is unavailable, the assistant should not invent an arrival time. It can state what is stale, show the last verified schedule, offer a direct timetable, and suggest checking the operator. Graceful failure preserves the person's ability to act even when the model cannot answer.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Cause a low-stakes AI product to fail or refuse. Does it name the actual boundary? Is your work preserved? Does the next action change the conditions of success? Can you distinguish feedback from help with your current outcome? If repeated generation is the main recovery, the product has delegated error handling to you.

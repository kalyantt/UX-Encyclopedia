Make a Research and Discovery Plan

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Make a Research and Discovery Plan.

Guide: A discovery plan is a route from uncertainty to action. Begin with decisions and their dates:

Plan backward from decisions

Host: What changes when we look at plan backward from decisions?

Guide: choose or revise the problem frame; select among intervention families; include or exclude a population, channel, or use; commit to data, policy, service, or technical architecture; prototype, pilot, expand, pause, or stop; accept, mitigate, or escalate risk.

Guide: For each decision, identify owner, contributors, consequence if wrong, assumptions, existing evidence, remaining uncertainty, evidence threshold, and likely response. If no plausible result can change the decision, the activity is not decision research. It may be monitoring, stakeholder engagement, or documentation—and should be named honestly.

Turn assumptions into research questions

Host: What changes when we look at turn assumptions into research questions?

Guide: Avoid vague goals such as “understand clinic choice.” Ask questions tied to a mechanism:

Guide: How do patients currently distinguish eligibility, service facts, inference, and promotion? Which clinic facts actually change choice or produce recovery when wrong? How are those facts created, owned, updated, and contested? What happens under proxy, non-digital, assistive-technology, and low-capacity clinic conditions?

Guide: Which source-state presentation supports appropriate confidence and action? Can proposed owners complete verification under realistic workload? What outcome and guardrail pattern would justify a bounded pilot?

Guide: Separate exploratory, descriptive, evaluative, causal, and monitoring questions. Each needs different evidence.

Match method to claim

Host: What changes when we look at match method to claim?

Guide: Use methods for what they can establish:

Guide: Interviews: meaning, past episodes, expectations, language, consequence, and reported behaviour. Contextual inquiry/observation: actual work, tacit judgement, interruption, tools, workarounds, and environment. Diary or longitudinal study: events and change that are difficult to observe directly.

Guide: Content/data audit: source quality, duplication, missingness, lifecycle, and structure. Journey or service mapping: actors, handoffs, waits, ownership, and systemic opportunity. Concept testing: comprehension and perceived relevance of alternative value propositions.

Guide: Usability/accessibility evaluation: task performance, understanding, error, recovery, and inclusive operation under tested conditions. Technical spike/simulation: integration, latency, data, reliability, and operational feasibility. Survey: distribution of clearly defined attitudes or self-reports in a defensible sample.

Guide: Experiment: comparative causal effect under explicit assignment, exposure, measures, and assumptions. Telemetry/analytics: recorded behaviour and distribution, interpreted with instrumentation and selection limits.

Guide: Triangulate when one method cannot support the decision. Do not collect multiple weak signals merely to say methods were mixed.

Design the sample around relevant variation

Host: What changes when we look at design the sample around relevant variation?

Guide: “Users” are not one participant group. Recruit conditions that can change the mechanism:

Guide: first versus repeat referral; access or scheduling need; digital versus supported/non-digital route; self versus proxy action; language and literacy context; clinic size, governance capacity, region, and integration maturity; coordinator expertise and workload; successful, failed, corrected, and abandoned cases.

Guide: Sampling is a claim boundary. A small qualitative sample can expose mechanisms and severe problems; it does not estimate population prevalence. Purposefully include rare high-consequence cases rather than allowing traffic frequency to define importance.

Build ethics and operations into the plan

Host: What changes when we look at build ethics and operations into the plan?

Guide: Specify recruitment access, informed consent, sensitive topics, participant support, withdrawal, recording, storage, retention, tool/vendor use, anonymisation limits, and compensation. Plan accommodations and alternate participation; do not exclude people because the research setup is inaccessible.

Guide: Account for recruitment lead time, clinic permission, staff workload, researcher capacity, prototype readiness, instrumentation, and analysis. Protect time for complete-case reading, contradiction, and peer review. Fast research is credible when scope and claims are bounded, not when analysis is removed.

Define disconfirming evidence and stopping rules

Host: What changes when we look at define disconfirming evidence and stopping rules?

Guide: Write what would weaken the frame or concept:

Guide: Predetermine revise, narrow, escalate, or stop responses. This prevents research from becoming an elaborate confirmation ritual.

Synthesis is part of the plan

Host: What changes when we look at synthesis is part of the plan?

Guide: Define how observations become claims: recordings and notes, evidence units, coding or structured comparison, counterexamples, analyst memos, confidence, and decision brief. Preserve observation → interpretation → implication → recommendation.

Guide: Share emerging evidence with limitations while it can still alter work. The final output should state supported and prohibited claims, not just themes. Store reusable evidence with consent and provenance; a repository is not useful if later teams cannot judge method and context.

Transfer example: payment failure

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: payment failure?

Guide: A team has two weeks to choose between clearer error content, retry changes, and alternate payment. The plan combines funnel/error telemetry, support-case review, observation of failed tasks, provider technical investigation, and prototype recovery testing. Each evidence stream answers a different decision.

Guide: Generic interviews about checkout satisfaction would not locate the mechanism.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Method calendar: activities are scheduled without a decision they can change. Question–claim mismatch: interviews estimate prevalence or analytics claim motivation. Recruitment convenience: easy participants exclude the context where consequence is highest. Happy-path stimulus: prototypes omit uncertainty, policy, latency, and recovery.

Guide: Confirmation architecture: only supporting results have a defined response. Ethics as paperwork: consent exists while data reuse, distress, or exclusion remains unmanaged. Report endpoint: findings have an audience but no owner, threshold, or next action. Analysis compression: speed is achieved by removing contradiction and contextual judgement.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior researchers protect decision quality under constraint. They narrow claims before weakening method, make organisational feasibility a research question, and refuse activities whose result cannot influence a choice. They negotiate timing so evidence arrives before irreversible commitment and ensure high-consequence minority contexts are not averaged away.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a clinic scheduling service. A team must decide whether to build self-service rescheduling. Its plan names the decision owner and deadline, observes current work, interviews people who abandoned the flow, checks staff capacity, and tests a simple alternative. Each method is chosen for the uncertainty it can reduce while the decision is still changeable.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Read a research plan and underline every method. For each, locate the decision, claim, participant boundary, evidence threshold, and response. If you cannot, rewrite one activity from “conduct interviews” into a question whose possible answers lead to different actions.

Feedforward, Feedback, and System Status

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Feedforward, Feedback, and System Status.

Guide: Feedforward explains what can happen next: an action’s consequence, requirement, or availability. Feedback explains what happened after an action. System status explains the current state while work is ongoing or after it changes. Each reduces uncertainty at a different moment.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Design all three around the task. A destructive control needs feedforward; a completed upload needs feedback; a long-running export needs persistent status. Make messages durable when the consequence is durable, and keep the state visible where people will look for it later.

Explain possibility before action and reality after it

Host: What changes when we look at explain possibility before action and reality after it?

Guide: Feedforward helps people predict what an action will do; feedback acknowledges input; status represents the resulting state over time. These functions are related but not interchangeable. A tooltip that predicts “Send request” cannot replace evidence that the service received it, and a success animation cannot justify a state the backend does not know.

Transfer example: bank transfer

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: bank transfer?

Guide: “Pay” needs feedforward about recipient, amount, fees, and timing. A spinner gives feedback. A receipt and transaction status provide evidence. Collapsing all three into a green tick can create serious false belief.

Design the action loop

Host: What changes when we look at design the action loop?

Guide: Feedforward explains what action is possible and what will happen. Feedback acknowledges input and shows resulting state. System status explains ongoing work, ownership, time, and next action. Together they let a person predict, act, interpret, and recover.

Guide: Match prominence and timing to consequence. Hover affordance is insufficient for touch and keyboard. A destructive control should name its effect before action. Immediate input needs acknowledgement within perceptual expectation; delayed work needs honest progress and the ability to continue elsewhere. Completion should state what changed, not merely say “Success.”

Guide: Handle partial and uncertain states. Distinguish saved locally, synced, submitted, approved, and published. Announce dynamic changes to assistive technology without overwhelming. Avoid optimistic UI where a false success could cause costly repeat or loss.

Guide: Evaluate the complete loop by asking people to predict the result and explain current state. Errors often come from a broken transition, not a poorly drawn component.

Build a status contract for every consequential action

Host: What changes when we look at build a status contract for every consequential action?

Guide: Feedforward appears before the boundary of commitment. The transfer review names clinic, information shared, protection of the existing appointment, and uncertainty. Feedback confirms that the system received input and prevents accidental repetition. Persistent status then shows the request object, latest trustworthy event, source, time, responsible actor, and next step.

Guide: Each layer answers a different question.

Guide: Latency is designed according to what the system knows. Short local work may need subtle inline feedback. Distributed review needs durable state and resumption. A timeout after submission becomes “checking whether your request was received,” not a confident failure and retry. Indeterminate progress is honest; fabricated percentages are not.

Guide: Notifications, in-product history, staff tools, and support share the event model. Out-of-order delivery, stale caches, partial failure, and background completion are tested. The team measures duplicate action, false belief, unresolved waiting, recovery, and support handoff. A delightful animation cannot compensate for an unsupported success claim.

Transfer example: payment

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: payment?

Guide: Before payment, feedforward shows merchant, amount, fees, and timing. A pressed state acknowledges input. Authorisation, settlement, and refund are different statuses with different evidence. One green tick cannot safely represent all of them.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Transient confirmation is not adequate for consequential work. If a person must later prove what happened, preserve the evidence in the relevant object, history, or notification.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Design feedback as a time sequence. Before action, make outcome, requirement, and reversibility visible. During work, show accepted, pending, blocked, or failed state and what the person can safely do. After work, preserve durable evidence when the outcome affects money, identity, publication, data, or another person.

Guide: Distinguish local acknowledgement from confirmed service completion.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: False completion: a toast implies success while the underlying operation is still pending or failed. Silent latency: people cannot tell whether to wait, retry, cancel, or seek help. Ephemeral receipt: consequential evidence disappears before it can be checked later. Status without action: a problem is displayed without a safe recovery route.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Before pressing “Submit,” a person cannot tell whether they will send a draft or publish it. After pressing, the page flashes a toast but the status is unchanged. The system communicated neither consequence nor result reliably.

What UX is

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about What UX is. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: A polished screen can announce success while the person is still unsure whether anything useful happened. To judge the experience, follow the entire attempt: the goal, the action, the service response, and what the person can do next.

What this moment reveals

Host: What changes when we look at what this moment reveals?

Guide: User experience is what people perceive and how they respond as they use a product or service in pursuit of a goal. It unfolds before, during, and after an interaction. An interface contributes to that experience, but so do the information a person receives beforehand, the reliability of the underlying service, and the consequences of what happens next.

Guide: For design work, the useful unit is often the attempt: what someone was trying to do, what happened, what effort or risk it involved, and whether the outcome made sense to them. A screen is one moment in that attempt. Judging the screen alone can miss the larger failure, just as judging an entire service by one bad button can miss the specific repair it needs.

Guide: Interaction design gives this idea a practical scope: design interactive products that support how people work, communicate, and live. It is broader than arranging controls. It draws on psychology, ergonomics, graphic and product design, engineering, information architecture, social research, and increasingly accessibility, privacy, ethics, and AI.

Guide: No single specialist owns the experience; a design decision is only as good as the combined human, business, technical, and operational understanding behind it.

Guide: When assessing UX, start with the activity rather than the interface. What is the person trying to achieve? What prior knowledge do they bring? What counts as a safe outcome? What could go wrong? What should be true when they finish? These questions prevent a team from polishing a moment while missing the actual job.

Follow the mechanism

Host: Can we follow the mechanism step by step?

Guide: One useful diagnostic model is: Goal → action → system response → outcome → interpretation → next action. This is a teaching lens for tracing an experience, not a claim that every book defines UX as this exact sequence.

Guide: Goal: “What am I here to accomplish?” Action: “Can I tell what to do?” Response: “Did the system explain what happened?” Outcome: “Did I make progress?” Interpretation: “Do I feel confident, annoyed, safe, or fooled?” Next action: “Would I continue or abandon?”

Guide: The loop matters because people judge a product through results and meaning, not through pixels in isolation.

Guide: The five layers you must inspect

Guide: 1. Purpose and user need. A product must support a real activity or need; it cannot be judged by visual refinement alone. 2. Interaction. People need to understand available actions, make a choice, and receive feedback that they can interpret. 3. Information and interface. Language, hierarchy, controls, timing, and visual structure make the interaction possible. 4.

Guide: System and service. Data, policies, people, logistics, and technical reliability determine whether a promised action actually succeeds. 5. Experience and consequence. The person leaves with an outcome and an interpretation: relief, competence, uncertainty, trust, frustration, or harm.

Guide: These layers interact. A control can be easy to operate while the resulting service action is wrong. A transaction can complete correctly while its feedback leaves the person unsure whether it is safe to continue. That is why a whole-system view matters, even when the eventual repair is local.

Use the lens in a design review

Host: What changes when we look at use the lens in a design review?

Guide: Start with the person's goal and the consequence of getting it wrong. Trace the action, the system's response, and the actual outcome separately. Then ask what the person could reasonably infer from the information available to them. Do not treat a completion event as proof that the goal was achieved or understood.

Guide: Write an experience hypothesis before choosing a solution: “For [a person in a situation], this change should help them [make progress on a goal] by [supporting an activity], without [material cost or risk].” List the interaction, service, and organisational conditions required for that claim to hold.

Guide: This reveals when a clearer message is enough and when the underlying service must change.

Evaluate the experience at three resolutions

Host: What changes when we look at evaluate the experience at three resolutions?

Guide: A whole-system view does not mean every design discussion must include everything. Senior judgement includes choosing the right resolution without losing the connections between them.

Guide: At the moment level, inspect one exchange. Is the action discoverable? Does the response describe what happened? Can the person recover from a mistake?

Guide: At the journey level, inspect connected episodes over time. Does information survive transitions? Do policies contradict one another? Can the person resume after an interruption? Does one step prepare them for the next?

Guide: At the system level, inspect the conditions that make the journey possible: capacity, staff roles, data standards, identity, consent, business rules, vendor systems, accessibility, governance, and incentives. A reminder cannot create unavailable capacity. A clearer error message cannot repair a handoff that nobody owns.

Guide: Interface improvements still matter, but the team must be honest about the layer at which they operate.

Guide: Use all three resolutions in critique. Ask what happens in the moment, what it does to the journey, and what system condition produces it. Then make the scope explicit. Sometimes the responsible decision is a small interface fix. Sometimes the interface is revealing a service failure that needs a different owner.

Guide: The point is not to make every project enormous; it is to avoid confusing the visible surface with the experienced problem.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Treating UX as a visual-polish phase after the “real” product work. Measuring a click instead of whether the person reached a meaningful outcome. Calling every moment “delightful” when the person mainly needs speed, certainty, or recovery. Designing the happy path and forgetting error states, waiting, handoffs, and support.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: When a team debates a screen, bring the discussion back to the person’s goal, cost of failure, and the whole service that shapes the outcome.

Guide: Senior practitioners choose the right resolution for the decision. They can correct a misleading confirmation without claiming to redesign an entire service, while documenting the state or ownership problem the interface cannot solve. They connect local craft to system consequence and ensure outcome ownership crosses organisational boundaries.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: airline check-in. A traveller tries to check in on a phone while carrying luggage and using an unreliable connection. The form accepts the details and displays “Done.” That response is only one event.

Guide: The traveller still needs to know whether the boarding pass was issued, whether a document check remains, and what to do if the connection drops before the pass is saved. At the moment level, examine the wording and feedback after submission. At the journey level, examine whether the pass is available later in the app, email, or airport kiosk.

Guide: At the system level, examine whether document rules and staff processes agree with what the app promised. A visually elegant confirmation cannot compensate for a missing boarding pass; a plain confirmation can work well if it gives durable, trustworthy proof and a recovery route. The example is hypothetical.

Guide: It illustrates the distinction between system response, real outcome, and the person's interpretation; it is not a claim about a particular airline.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: This opening lesson defines the experience attempt. lesson 2 tests that definition against usability, and the remaining foundation lessons add emotion, context, service, and trade-offs.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: During your next app session, stop immediately after a system response: a payment confirmation, saved-state message, loading state, or error. Ask: What does this response make me believe happened, and what evidence does it give me?

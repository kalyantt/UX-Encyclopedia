Contextual Inquiry and Field Observation

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Contextual Inquiry and Field Observation. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Field observation studies behaviour in the setting where it occurs. Contextual inquiry adds a collaborative posture: observe real work, ask about actions as they happen, and let the participant explain their own practice. It exposes physical conditions, social coordination, workarounds, artifacts, interruptions, and invisible handoffs that interviews may not surface.

Observe work in its real ecology

Host: What changes when we look at observe work in its real ecology?

Guide: Notes capture environment, tools, artefacts, sequence, exceptions, collaboration, and hidden emotional or cognitive load. Consent is ongoing, sensitive records are protected, and observation is designed not to disrupt care. The team later validates interpretations with staff and distinguishes one local practice from a systemic pattern.

Failure modes in practice

Host: What changes when we look at failure modes in practice?

Guide: Workplace tour: the researcher sees tools but not complete episodes or exceptions. Procedure bias: official instructions are recorded as though they describe practice. Workaround removal: an awkward step is deleted before its protective function is understood. Calm-day sampling: routine conditions hide interruption, overload, and recovery.

Guide: Invisible observer effect: changed behavior and unobservable work are omitted from limitations.

Guide: Observe respectfully. Obtain consent, protect sensitive information, avoid disrupting critical work, and distinguish the task from your interpretation. Capture sequences, tools, language, constraints, breakdowns, and adaptations. Afterwards, compare what you observed with what people say and what systems record; disagreement is a research finding, not an inconvenience.

Observe activity in context

Host: What changes when we look at observe activity in context?

Guide: Go where the work occurs when safe and appropriate. Establish a partnership: the participant is expert in their practice while the researcher asks, observes, and interprets. Follow real tasks, interruptions, artefacts, communication, environment, timing, and exceptions. Ask about actions after they occur rather than turning the session into an interview detached from behaviour.

Guide: Record sequence, objects, information, people, breakdowns, adaptation, and consequence. Note what cannot be observed and how researcher presence may change behaviour. In sensitive settings, minimise intrusion, protect bystanders, and create clear stopping and data-handling rules.

Guide: Use interpretation checks without asking participants to validate every analytic conclusion. Compare formal procedure with actual practice and distinguish skilful workaround from avoidable burden. Field evidence often exposes that the unit of design is a workflow or service, not one screen.

Guide: Synthesise across cases and contexts. Observation provides depth and mechanism; combine it with other evidence to assess reach and outcome.

Turn observation into a model of work

Host: What changes when we look at turn observation into a model of work?

Guide: Workarounds are analysed by function. A spreadsheet may preserve state missing from the clinical system; a personal message may secure a response when the formal queue has no owner. Removing either can harm the service. The team records the trigger, value, risk, dependency, and who bears the work, then decides whether to support, redesign, or eliminate the underlying need.

Guide: Observation is sampled across normal and difficult cases, staff roles, times, locations, and levels of demand. The team validates its model with participants and compares it with policy and logs. The output is a work model and breakdown map that changes system requirements, not a list of individual efficiency criticisms.

Transfer example: warehouse picking

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: warehouse picking?

Guide: Workers may ignore a recommended route because shelving changes faster than the system updates. Observation reveals spatial cues, shared knowledge, and safety constraints that an interview room or clickstream cannot contain. Automating the official flow would institutionalise bad data.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Observation can expose organisational work that leaders have made invisible. Protect participants from surveillance, validate interpretations with them, and ask whether a workaround is compensating for policy, capacity, or poor tooling before attempting to standardise it.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: At a warehouse desk, a worker says the inventory tool is fine. Watching a shift reveals printed cheat sheets, an unofficial sequence of tabs, a scanner held in one hand, and a manager interrupting every exception. The work is richer than the retrospective account.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Watch a familiar task being completed in its real setting. Record interruptions, artefacts, handoffs, and workarounds that would disappear in a conference-room description of the same task.

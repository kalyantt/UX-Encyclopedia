Prototypes Answer Questions

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Prototypes Answer Questions.

Guide: A prototype is a partial representation of a possible future, built so someone can experience, inspect, or enact it before the team commits to the full system. It is not automatically a miniature product.

Guide: A storyboard, paper form, clickable flow, role-play, data simulation, policy draft, coded interaction, or concierge service can all be prototypes when they create evidence for a decision.

A prototype is a question made tangible

Host: What changes when we look at a prototype is a question made tangible?

Guide: That separates a prototype from a mock-up. A mock-up may communicate appearance. A prototype must make a relevant question answerable. “Do stakeholders like it?” is usually too vague. Better questions concern behaviour or consequence: Can a patient distinguish two specialties? What information changes the choice? Can staff supply it reliably?

Guide: What happens when no suitable appointment exists?

Guide: Fidelity is often mistaken for visual polish. In practice it has independent dimensions:

Guide: Visual fidelity: typography, colour, spacing, imagery, and resemblance to the intended interface. Behavioural fidelity: whether controls, state changes, delays, errors, and recovery behave credibly. Content and data fidelity: whether names, prices, clinical descriptions, availability, and exceptions are realistic.

Guide: Breadth: how much of the journey and how many paths are represented. Technical fidelity: whether the prototype uses real integrations, devices, performance, or production code. Social and operational fidelity: whether staff roles, handoffs, policies, and environments are enacted.

Guide: A prototype may be visually rough but behaviourally credible. Conversely, a beautiful clickable screen can be low fidelity where it matters because every appointment is instantly available and every patient follows the happy path.

Choose the cheapest credible representation

Host: What changes when we look at choose the cheapest credible representation?

Guide: Start with a decision, not a tool.

Guide: 1. Name the decision. What will the team do differently after learning? If no plausible result changes a decision, the prototype is theatre. 2. Name the uncertainty. Is the risk about desirability, comprehension, sequence, accessibility, capacity, feasibility, trust, or outcomes? 3. Specify the claim. Write what the prototype can and cannot support.

Guide: “This can reveal whether participants understand the distinction in this scenario; it cannot estimate prevalence or prove clinical safety.” 4. Choose the representation. Use the least expensive form that preserves the conditions needed for a meaningful response. 5. Model consequential states.

Guide: Include delays, missing information, error, recovery, and handoffs when they could change the decision. 6. Capture evidence. Record behaviour, interpretation, breakdowns, workarounds, and questions—not only preference. 7. Decide and retire. Advance, revise, reject, or investigate. Do not let an exploratory artefact quietly become production.

Guide: “Cheapest credible” matters. Cheap but unbelievable produces false evidence. Credible but unnecessarily expensive slows learning and raises attachment to the idea.

Transfer example: a delivery promise

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: a delivery promise?

Guide: An ecommerce team wants to test “arrives tomorrow.” A static screen can test whether the promise is noticed, but not whether customers trust it after stock changes. A coded slice using inventory data might be needed for technical feasibility. A service rehearsal with support may be needed to test recovery when the promise is broken.

Guide: These prototypes answer different questions; more visual fidelity would not make them interchangeable.

What prototypes cannot prove

Host: What changes when we look at what prototypes cannot prove?

Guide: A prototype session samples a constructed encounter. It does not by itself prove market demand, long-term habit, operational scalability, accessibility conformance, safety, or business viability. Participants may be unusually attentive, the moderator may help unintentionally, and simulated stakes may change behaviour.

Guide: Treat findings as evidence bounded by representation and context.

Guide: Common failures include:

Guide: Questionless construction: screens are built because prototyping is expected. Fidelity theatre: polish is mistaken for validity or readiness. Happy-path evidence: errors, waiting, permissions, and recovery are removed. Prototype leakage: temporary code, fake data assumptions, or inaccessible components become production.

Guide: Attachment cost: effort makes the concept harder to reject. Unlabelled omissions: observers infer that absent policy, content, or engineering is solved.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior practitioners manage what an artefact causes an organisation to believe. Before review, state the question, deliberately real dimensions, simulated dimensions, and out-of-scope claims. Ask engineering, operations, content, accessibility, legal, and domain experts which conditions must be credible.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a parcel-tracking service. The team is unsure whether people understand the difference between “label created” and “collected.” A clickable prototype needs only the status timeline, explanatory copy, and a help route. It does not need a finished visual system. The prototype is successful if it reveals what people infer and what they would do next.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Find a product demo or prototype. Ignore how finished it looks. Identify the question it can answer, one important condition it simulates poorly, and one conclusion a stakeholder might incorrectly draw.

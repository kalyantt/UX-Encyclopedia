Prototype the Whole Service

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Prototype the Whole Service. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Service prototypes rehearse the combined experience of people, policy, environment, technology, and communications. They may use role-play, concierge delivery, walkthroughs, mock artefacts, simulations, or small pilots. Their goal is to learn how the whole service behaves before expensive commitment.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Prototype the riskiest service assumptions, including handoffs and exceptions. Observe both customer and staff experience. A service is ready only when the promise, operations, and recovery paths can work together.

Choose fidelity for the service risk

Host: What changes when we look at choose fidelity for the service risk?

Guide: Service-prototype fidelity has several dimensions: visual representation, behavioural realism, data realism, role realism, policy authority, environmental conditions, and operational load. Raising all of them at once is expensive and can create false confidence.

Guide: The team should make only the dimensions needed for the current decision realistic and state clearly what remains simulated.

Guide: Findings determine the next representation. If the state model fails, revise and rehearse it. If supplier acknowledgement is uncertain, run a technical spike. If capacity is the risk, simulate realistic volume.

Guide: If people understand the process but live outcomes remain uncertain, move to a limited pilot with eligibility boundaries, monitoring, rollback, and pre-agreed stop conditions. Progression is based on retired uncertainty, not visual polish.

Transfer example: airport assistance

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: airport assistance?

Guide: A screen prototype can test booking assistance, but not whether information reaches the airline, ground handler, security, and gate staff or whether equipment and staff are available during disruption. Role-play in the physical environment with timed handoffs may reveal the decisive failure before any software is built.

Prototype orchestration and failure

Host: What changes when we look at prototype orchestration and failure?

Guide: Create a scenario that crosses channels and roles. Represent customer actions, staff decisions, messages, records, queues, policy, and time. A paper form, role-play, mocked notification, live spreadsheet, and simulated call can together reveal more than a polished front-end prototype.

Guide: Test the promise under realistic load and exception. Does information follow the person? Can staff recognise state and act? Does capacity match demand? What happens when a supplier, system, or person is unavailable? Include recovery and escalation rather than ending at the successful handoff.

Guide: Measure both sides: customer progress, understanding, effort, and dignity; staff workload, error, authority, and coordination. Record which elements were simulated and what requires a live pilot. Service prototyping reduces operational and policy risk before scale, not just usability risk.

Prototype the risky relationships

Host: What changes when we look at prototype the risky relationships?

Guide: He stages a role-play with realistic messages, staff scripts, simulated data latency, a manual eligibility check, and failure injection. Participants encounter a disappearing slot and conflicting confirmation while team members operate the backstage process. The prototype records waiting, handoffs, workload, misunderstanding, and recovery, not only task completion.

Guide: Fidelity follows uncertainty. Paper and role-play are enough for workflow and responsibility; coded integration may be needed for timing or data behavior. Safety boundaries prevent a prototype from being mistaken for live clinical service. Findings update the blueprint, operating procedure, product design, and measurement plan together.

Guide: Whole-service prototyping reduces the risk of shipping a polished interface attached to an unworkable operation.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: The cheapest prototype is often a rehearsal of policy and people, not a high-fidelity interface. Choose the representation that answers the decision at risk.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Choose a service prototype that tests the riskiest system behaviour. Role-play can reveal handoff and conversation issues; a concierge approach can test demand and manual operations; a simulated environment can test staff workflows; a limited pilot can test capacity, policy, and real customer expectations.

Guide: Represent the parts necessary for valid learning, including communications, waiting, data, authority, and recovery—not only the digital screen.

Guide: Observe both sides of the service. Ask what the customer understands and does, what staff need to know and decide, how long work takes, what breaks under variation, and where responsibility becomes unclear. Define safety limits and stop conditions before a live pilot.

Guide: A prototype that delights a customer while exhausting staff or relying on unsustainable manual work has revealed a problem, not validated a service.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Screen-only prototype: the UI is tested without the people, policy, or operations needed to keep its promise. Manual magic: concierge effort is treated as proof that automation or scale will work. No exception rehearsal: normal flow succeeds but recovery has never been tested. Customer-only evaluation: staff burden, capacity, and operational errors remain invisible.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A new appointment interface is tested successfully. On launch, staff lack training, rooms are unavailable, and cancellation policy conflicts with the on-screen promise. The screen was prototyped; the service was not.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Co-creation builds a shared service model. Whole-service prototyping tests it. Module 11 focuses on prototypes and evaluation across product work.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Design a low-cost rehearsal for a service change without building software. What would it reveal about people, policy, or handoffs?

Qualitative Analysis: From Raw Evidence to Traceable Themes

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Qualitative Analysis: From Raw Evidence to Traceable Themes. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Qualitative analysis moves deliberately from raw evidence to defensible patterns. Preserve the source: observations, quotes, artifacts, and context. Code meaningful fragments, compare them across participants, look for recurrence and variation, and assemble themes that explain a mechanism rather than merely repeat a complaint.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Traceability matters. A theme should lead back to evidence; an insight should state what the pattern means; an implication should name what the team might change or investigate. Avoid turning frequency into proof of importance or a vivid anecdote into prevalence. Look actively for disconfirming cases.

Analyse relationships, variation, and negative cases

Host: What changes when we look at analyse relationships, variation, and negative cases?

Guide: Themes state a relationship and scope, cite supporting cases, preserve negative cases, and distinguish observation from interpretation. A matrix compares referral state, route, clinic, and outcome. The final claim can be traced to source material under appropriate access while participant identity remains protected.

Create an auditable analysis path

Host: What changes when we look at create an auditable analysis path?

Guide: Define the unit of analysis—episode, statement, behaviour, decision, or case—and retain participant and context. Familiarise before fragmenting. Develop codes through close reading, document definitions and changes, and compare how codes operate across people and situations.

Guide: Move from codes to candidate themes by asking what shared mechanism explains the evidence. Review within-theme coherence and between-theme distinction. Search for negative cases, alternative explanations, and missing perspectives. Frequency can orient attention but does not determine importance.

Guide: Maintain a matrix or repository linking themes to excerpts, cases, analytic memos, and research questions. Separate what participants said, what researchers observed, and what the team inferred. Collaborative review should expose different interpretations rather than vote them away.

Guide: Report scope, evidence strength, contradiction, and consequence. A traceable theme allows another person to inspect the reasoning and supports later reanalysis when the product or question changes.

Build an auditable analysis trail

Host: What changes when we look at build an auditable analysis trail?

Guide: Codes can be descriptive—“calls clinic,” “availability unknown”—or interpretive—“creates independent verification.” The team distinguishes them. It compares within a case before comparing across cases, then uses matrices to examine referral state, channel, access condition, clinic, and outcome.

Guide: Frequency helps identify recurrence in the sample but does not automatically establish prevalence or importance; one rare safety failure may matter more than a common preference.

Guide: Theme development asks what relationship explains the evidence. “Uncertainty” is a topic. “When status lacks an accountable actor and expected time, patients create verification work that increases service demand” is an explanatory theme. The team searches for negative cases, alternative mechanisms, and conditions where the pattern does not hold.

Guide: The final theme record links claims to excerpts and cases, states scope and confidence, and names the product decision it informs.

Transfer example: workplace approvals

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: workplace approvals?

Guide: Interview notes repeatedly mention email, but the mechanism is not “people prefer email.” Analysis shows that employees use forwarded messages as durable evidence when the approval system hides history. The design implication is inspectable state and audit, not another notification preference.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Do not code toward a desired answer. Include a “what would change our interpretation?” pass before presenting themes.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Keep an audit trail from each theme to source material, coding decisions, and counterexamples. Compare cases across role, task, context, and time; a repeated pattern may have different mechanisms in different situations. Use memoing to capture interpretations as they emerge, then revisit them with fresh evidence and collaborators.

Guide: The goal is not the largest cluster of notes but the clearest explanation that can support a product decision.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Theme by volume: the most frequent statement is assumed to be the most consequential finding. Premature convergence: a label is applied before competing explanations have been examined. Lost traceability: stakeholders cannot inspect the evidence behind a conclusion. Erased exception: a smaller but important experience is forced into the dominant theme.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Ten interviews produce a wall of notes. “Users want a simpler dashboard” becomes the headline, but nobody can point to the exact behaviours, contexts, contradictions, or participants behind it. The finding is memorable and unusable.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Take five product reviews. Label observation, context, need, workaround, and consequence. Form one tentative theme and cite the evidence beneath it.

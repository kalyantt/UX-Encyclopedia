Research Ethics

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Research Ethics.

Guide: Research ethics protects people’s dignity, agency, privacy, and safety. Participants need understandable information about purpose, activities, recording, data use, risks, compensation, and their right to stop. Consent is an ongoing choice, not a checkbox collected once.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Collect only what is necessary, protect it appropriately, and avoid exposing people to avoidable discomfort or consequences. Plan for power imbalances and sensitive contexts. An employee, patient, student, or financially vulnerable participant may not feel equally free to refuse. Ethical research improves evidence because people can participate knowingly and safely.

Ethics continues after the session

Host: What changes when we look at ethics continues after the session?

Guide: In synthesis, the team avoids turning one painful story into emotional theatre. Clips require permission and limited audience. Findings describe structural conditions without stereotyping people as vulnerable or noncompliant. When research identifies a harmful status message, the team assigns an owner and interim protection rather than waiting for the final report.

Design protection through the whole research lifecycle

Host: What changes when we look at design protection through the whole research lifecycle?

Guide: Risk is situational. Discussing delayed care may cause distress; a helper may reveal another person’s information; screen recordings may capture medical data; staff may fear performance consequences. The plan includes stop rules, signposting, secure handling, role-limited access, anonymisation limits, and a route for unexpected safety concerns.

Guide: Participants learn how findings were used; lived experience is not extracted as decorative evidence.

Design participant protection

Host: What changes when we look at design participant protection?

Guide: Assess purpose, necessity, vulnerability, power, sensitivity, burden, potential harm, and benefit. Make participation voluntary with understandable consent, ability to pause or withdraw, proportionate incentive, accessible materials, and honest explanation of recording and data use.

Guide: Minimise collection and access. Define storage, retention, deletion, de-identification limits, vendors, and incident response before sessions. Protect confidentiality in notes, clips, repositories, and stakeholder presentations. Distinctive stories can re-identify even without names.

Guide: Plan for distress, disclosure, unsafe situations, and research that uncovers product harm. Researchers need escalation and duty-of-care guidance. Ethics continues through analysis and storytelling: avoid stereotyping, extractive participation, fabricated certainty, and recommendations that increase burden on people with least power.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Ethical risk should influence method choice and study scope. A fast study is not a success if it creates harm, captures data the organisation cannot steward, or produces consent people did not understand.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Ethics is embedded in the study design. Choose tasks, questions, recording, and storage practices that are proportionate to the benefit of learning. Explain what will happen in language people can understand, avoid collecting sensitive material “just in case,” and separate participation from any service, employment, education, or payment decision that could create pressure.

Guide: When research involves trauma, health, financial hardship, identity, or legal status, build in specialist review, support, and safer alternatives.

Guide: Consent must remain meaningful throughout the session. A person should be able to skip a question, stop recording, withdraw, or take a break without being penalised. Protect confidentiality in recruitment, observation, analysis, reporting, and playback. Share findings in a way that preserves context without making an individual identifiable to people who do not need to know.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Checkbox consent: a dense form replaces a genuine explanation and choice. Coercive context: participants believe service or employment depends on cooperation. Data excess: recordings and identifiers are retained without a defined need or owner. Extractive research: people share difficult experiences but receive no respect, support, or influence.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A diary study of financial hardship can create privacy and emotional risk even when no medical data is involved. Minimising prompts, enabling skips, separating incentives from completion, and planning deletion are research-design decisions.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Sampling decides who is included. Ethics determines how the team earns the right to learn from them. lesson 24 then teaches how to state the resulting evidence with appropriate confidence.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Review a sign-up flow. What would a participant need to know before agreeing to a study or data collection activity?

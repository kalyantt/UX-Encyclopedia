Evidence Has Confidence Levels

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Evidence Has Confidence Levels.

Guide: Evidence has levels of confidence. Separate direct observation from interpretation, interpretation from inference, and inference from recommendation. State what was seen, who it involved, under what conditions, and what alternative explanations remain.

Guide: This makes research more useful, not less persuasive: decision-makers can see what is known, what is plausible, and what needs another test.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Triangulation can strengthen confidence when different evidence sources point to the same explanation. It does not make uncertainty disappear. Sample limits, measurement quality, observer effects, novelty, and changing context all affect what a claim can support.

Match action to uncertainty and consequence

Host: What changes when we look at match action to uncertainty and consequence?

Guide: Low-confidence evidence can justify action when the action is safe and reversible: repair obviously ambiguous copy, add monitoring, or run a limited pilot. It may also justify pausing when potential harm is severe. High confidence in a problem does not imply confidence in a solution.

Guide: The team can know the state is confusing while remaining uncertain whether a status explanation, notification, or human contact is best.

Guide: Language should preserve inference. “We observed” identifies direct evidence; “suggests” signals interpretation; “is associated with” avoids a causal claim; “caused” requires a design capable of supporting it. This precision lets decision-makers take proportionate action rather than reading every slide as certainty.

Calibrate confidence to evidence and consequence

Host: What changes when we look at calibrate confidence to evidence and consequence?

Guide: The team uses calibrated language: observed in this sample, supported across sources, estimated with uncertainty, or untested. Conflicting evidence is investigated, not averaged into a vague middle. A high-consequence decision may require stronger evidence or a reversible pilot even when confidence seems reasonable.

Guide: The confidence register changes as new operational and user evidence arrives.

Calibrate confidence to source and consequence

Host: What changes when we look at calibrate confidence to source and consequence?

Guide: The team uses confidence to choose action, not to produce a decorative score. High-consequence, hard-to-reverse decisions require stronger and more diverse evidence. Lower-confidence findings may justify a safe prototype or monitoring.

Guide: Disconfirming cases remain visible, and every claim has an update trigger so old certainty does not survive changed policy or service conditions.

Calibrate claim and action

Host: What changes when we look at calibrate claim and action?

Guide: Assess directness, method fit, sample relevance, consistency, triangulation, measurement quality, alternative explanations, and consequence. Confidence is specific to a claim. Rich evidence of a severe failure can justify repair without estimating prevalence; a market-size decision needs different support.

Guide: Use language that preserves scope: observed, suggests, is associated, estimated, or caused under tested conditions. Report contradictory and missing evidence. Avoid colour scores without rationale; stakeholders need to know what would strengthen or overturn the claim.

Guide: Match action to confidence and reversibility. Weak but concerning evidence may justify a small safe test or pause, while strong evidence can support wider change. Do not demand certainty that research cannot provide, and do not let attractive anecdotes carry decisions beyond their evidential reach.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Overclaiming erodes trust faster than uncertainty does. Communicate confidence, limitations, and decision implications together: what should we do now, what would change our mind, and what risk remains?

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Give every claim a traceable chain: source material, observation, interpretation, inference, implication, and recommended action. The chain need not make a report cumbersome; it lets a team inspect the strength of a conclusion and revisit it when context changes. Label the boundary between what participants did or said and the team’s explanation of why it happened.

Guide: Confidence grows when evidence is direct, relevant, consistent, appropriately sampled, and plausible against alternative explanations. It falls when the context is artificial, the measure is weak, the sample is narrow, the behaviour may be influenced by the study, or the pattern depends on a single analyst’s interpretation.

Guide: Triangulation means comparing evidence with different strengths and blind spots; it is not averaging disagreement until it disappears.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Quote as proof: a vivid comment is treated as a universal pattern. Metric as diagnosis: a behavioural change is assumed to reveal its cause. Certainty inflation: tentative findings are turned into definitive roadmaps. Untraceable synthesis: nobody can find the material behind a conclusion.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Support tickets can prove that customers encounter account lockout, but cannot estimate the rate among all users without a denominator. Login logs provide scale but may not explain the human cause. Together they strengthen different parts of the claim.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: The research module ends by calibrating confidence. Module 5 turns this evidence into a continuous discovery practice rather than a one-time report.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Label three product-review claims as observation, opinion, interpretation, or evidence. What additional data would increase confidence?

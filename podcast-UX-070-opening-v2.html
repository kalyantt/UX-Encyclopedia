What an A/B Test Can Tell You

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: What an A/B Test Can Tell You.

Guide: To know a treatment’s causal effect for one person, we would need to observe their outcome both with and without it at the same time—an impossible counterfactual. A randomised controlled experiment instead assigns comparable units to control and treatment.

Guide: In expectation, known and unknown pre-treatment differences balance, so a difference in outcomes can be attributed to assignment with quantifiable uncertainty when the design and execution are sound.

Randomisation creates a counterfactual estimate

Host: What changes when we look at randomisation creates a counterfactual estimate?

Guide: The estimated effect is bounded. It applies to the eligible population, variants, implementation, period, and outcome definition studied. Randomisation does not prove why the effect occurred, whether it persists, whether excluded groups benefit, or whether the metric represents genuine value.

Guide: Assignment, exposure, and analysis are distinct. A patient may be assigned before ever reaching the changed explanation. Analyse according to the planned estimand: intention-to-treat estimates effect of assignment; triggered analyses may improve sensitivity for those with an opportunity to be exposed but require trustworthy triggering rules.

Design before looking at outcomes

Host: What changes when we look at design before looking at outcomes?

Guide: An experiment plan should specify:

Guide: decision and causal hypothesis; control and treatment, changing only what the claim requires; eligibility and exclusions; randomisation unit and persistence; exposure or trigger definition; primary metric, diagnostics, guardrails, and trust checks; minimum meaningful effect, sample, duration, and stopping rule; analysis method, segments, and multiple-testing policy; ethical review, ramp plan, rollback, and owner; limits on generalisation and follow-up evidence.

Guide: Choose the randomisation unit to avoid contamination and match how treatment operates. User-level assignment is common, but household, clinic, practitioner, region, or time cluster may be needed when people influence one another or operations cannot deliver both variants independently.

When not to run an A/B test

Host: What changes when we look at when not to run an a/b test?

Guide: Experiments are poor tools for discovering unmet needs, understanding a mental model, choosing an unformed concept, or studying rare harms without adequate exposure. Do not randomise people into known accessibility barriers, deception, or unsafe service. Some changes cannot coexist or have units too few for useful inference.

Guide: Use qualitative research, simulation, quasi-experimental methods, domain assurance, logs, or staged pilots as appropriate.

Transfer example: feature use and retention

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: feature use and retention?

Guide: Users who adopt a planning feature may retain better because already-engaged users are more likely to try it. Correlation cannot show that forcing adoption will improve retention. Random assignment of a credible invitation can estimate the effect of that invitation, not necessarily the effect of sustained feature use among compliers.

Guide: The intervention and estimand must match the decision.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Randomisation myth: random splitting is expected to repair weak outcomes or bad logging. Assignment/exposure confusion: people who never could encounter treatment dilute or distort interpretation. Unit mismatch: individual assignment ignores household, network, or operational interference. Metric fishing: outcomes or segments are selected after results appear.

Guide: Significance substitution: a small p-value replaces effect size and practical consequence. Generalisation leap: one context becomes a universal design law.

Match randomisation to the decision

Host: What changes when we look at match randomisation to the decision?

Guide: If the question concerns comprehension of a new status explanation, a controlled usability study may produce faster evidence. If the intervention changes clinic workflow, a phased or cluster design may be more credible. Experiments are one method in a learning portfolio; the method follows the causal question and service constraints.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: An experiment is an intervention on real people, not a neutral analytics task. Use equipoise: there should be genuine uncertainty between ethically acceptable variants. Ramp according to risk, monitor stop conditions, preserve recovery, and minimise data.

Guide: Senior practitioners separate strategy from optimisation; A/B tests can refine a direction but should not make organisational values invisible behind local metric movement.

Define the estimand before launching the variant

Host: What changes when we look at define the estimand before launching the variant?

Guide: Randomisation, sample-ratio checks, exposure logging, predeclared outcomes, guardrails, and sufficient duration protect inference. The test cannot explain mechanism by itself, guarantee transfer to another population, or legitimise a deceptive or unsafe variant. Qualitative evidence interprets why behaviour changed.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a streaming-service trial. An experiment randomly assigns eligible visitors to two descriptions of a trial offer. It can estimate the effect of that copy on sign-up for the tested population and period. It cannot, by itself, explain why people chose, prove long-term satisfaction, or justify hiding a future charge.

Guide: Those require other evidence and guardrails.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Find an A/B-test claim online. Rewrite it as: population, treatment, control, outcome, period, and estimated effect. Which missing field most weakens the original claim?

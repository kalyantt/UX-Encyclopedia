Colour, Contrast, and Redundant Cues

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Colour, Contrast, and Redundant Cues. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Colour is powerful because the visual system notices contrast and change quickly. But colour is contextual, not a reliable channel that carries a fixed meaning by itself. The ability to distinguish two colours depends on their lightness, saturation, size, separation, surroundings, display, and viewing conditions.

Guide: Small pale marks and thin text are particularly difficult to discriminate.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Design therefore begins with the meaning that must be perceived, not with a preferred palette. If a state means “requires action,” ask how that meaning survives when colour is unavailable, indistinct, or misunderstood. Pair colour with a label, icon, shape, pattern, position, status text, or changed control state.

Guide: Use contrast to establish hierarchy and legibility; do not assume contrast alone establishes a relationship or consequence.

Guide: This matters beyond accessibility. A dashboard that makes an outlier legible through colour plus a direct label is faster for everyone to scan. A form error marked with red plus a useful message gives people a diagnosis and recovery route. A selected navigation item that changes colour, weight, position, and state is easier to verify at a glance.

Guide: Do not turn this into “never use red and green.” The lesson is to treat colour as one signal in a deliberately redundant system. Also distinguish visual contrast from semantic contrast: a bright destructive button may be easy to see but still be ambiguous about what it destroys. The visual signal and the language must agree.

Visual comparison: when colour disappears

Host: What changes when we look at visual comparison: when colour disappears?

Guide: Imagine one compact chart shown twice. Version A distinguishes three categories only with nearly identical thin hues and a tiny legend. Version B uses a high-contrast palette, direct labels, line patterns, and a clearly marked outlier. Remove colour from both: only the second preserves category and outlier information. Redundancy is what makes the visual robust.

Mechanism and boundaries

Host: What changes when we look at mechanism and boundaries?

Guide: Colour perception depends on relationships, not isolated hex values. Apparent colour changes with surrounding colour, luminance, display, ambient light, viewing angle, and the size of the mark. Thin strokes and small text offer less signal than large regions.

Guide: Different colour-vision capabilities alter which hues can be discriminated, while ageing, glare, fatigue, and low-quality displays can reduce contrast for anyone.

Guide: Separate three jobs. Legibility needs sufficient contrast between foreground and background. Differentiation needs categories that can be told apart. Meaning needs an understandable mapping from appearance to state or action.

Guide: Passing a text contrast check does not prove that two chart series are distinguishable; distinguishable hues do not explain which series requires action; a red button does not explain what it will delete.

Guide: Design redundancy at the semantic layer. Pair colour with labels, position, shape, pattern, line style, iconography, and programmatic state as appropriate. Do not merely add an icon whose meaning also depends on colour. In charts, direct labels often reduce legend search. In forms, an error summary plus field-level message supports both discovery and correction.

Guide: For focus, selection, hover, visited, disabled, loading, warning, success, and failure, document the non-colour evidence.

Guide: Test in the component's real size and state, under zoom and reflow, with forced or high-contrast settings where relevant. Grayscale is a useful challenge but not a complete accessibility test. The goal is not a colourless interface; it is meaning that remains available when the colour channel weakens or disappears.

Treat colour as a governed semantic channel

Host: What changes when we look at treat colour as a governed semantic channel?

Guide: Contrast is checked across component states, not just static text: placeholder, disabled, focus, hover, error, selected, charts, overlays, and text on imagery. Tokens document intended combinations and prohibit unsafe pairings, while real pages test effects of opacity, layering, and adjacent colours.

Guide: Meeting a numeric threshold does not prove that dense or tiny content is readable.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Audit colour in states, not only in style guides. Ask what happens to loading, selected, disabled, warning, success, error, visited, and data-series states when someone cannot distinguish a hue, uses high zoom, sees the product outdoors, or consumes a screenshot in grayscale.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a train-platform alert. The platform changes from 4 to 7. Red text alone can be missed or misread. Place the new platform in the journey, pair colour with words and an icon, maintain contrast, and expose the update to assistive technology. Test whether travellers can identify the right platform under time pressure.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: lesson 8 explained that visual structure guides grouping; lesson 9 explained that attention is limited. This lesson makes one constraint concrete: salient colour is not automatically understandable colour. lesson 100 carries the same reasoning into reading, type, and scan paths.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Take a screenshot of a product with a status, chart, form error, or selected control. Temporarily remove colour mentally or with a grayscale filter. List every meaning that vanishes, then add the smallest second cue that restores it.

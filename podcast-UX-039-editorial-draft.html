Navigation and Wayfinding

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Navigation and Wayfinding. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Wayfinding helps people answer three questions: Where am I? What is here? Where can I go next? Navigation systems use global, local, contextual, utility, and in-content paths to make those answers available at the right scale.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Good navigation provides orientation without overwhelming a task. It reflects the information architecture, preserves context, gives meaningful labels, and lets people move laterally or recover from a wrong turn. Search does not replace navigation; both support different finding strategies.

Give people location, routes, and recovery

Host: What changes when we look at give people location, routes, and recovery?

Guide: Navigation offers possible movement; wayfinding helps a person understand where they are, what lies nearby, how they arrived, and how to continue or recover. Global, local, contextual, utility, and supplementary navigation serve different scopes.

Guide: A breadcrumb can express hierarchy, but it cannot explain a process state; a progress indicator can show sequence, but not where related evidence lives. The visible system should match the underlying relationship.

Guide: The team tests orientation after interruption, browser back, expired links, role switching, and changed state. It asks participants to point to where they are, predict what a navigation choice contains, return to a known place, and resume the task. It observes route efficiency but also confidence and false orientation.

Guide: A fast path to the wrong conceptual destination is not successful navigation.

Transfer example: public transport disruption

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: public transport disruption?

Guide: A passenger entering from a delay alert needs route, current segment, alternatives, and the effect on the final destination. Sending them to a generic network homepage preserves navigation chrome but destroys wayfinding context. The notification should land on a stateful journey view with a clear route back to alternatives.

Support orientation, route, and return

Host: What changes when we look at support orientation, route, and return?

Guide: Navigation should answer where am I, what is here, where can I go, what path did I take, and how do I return? Use persistent landmarks, clear current state, meaningful hierarchy, and local options suited to the task. Do not make people reconstruct context from browser history or memory.

Guide: Global, local, contextual, utility, and supplemental navigation serve different scopes. Search complements navigation when people know or can describe what they need. Cross-links should express meaningful relationships, not become undifferentiated “related content.”

Guide: Test deep links, back behaviour, role and permission changes, mobile layouts, long labels, and absent results. Tree testing isolates structure; task evaluation reveals how content and interface interact. Measure successful destination and recovery, not menu clicks.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Navigation choices redistribute attention and power. What is promoted, hidden, or named vaguely shapes what people can discover and what the business can steer.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Navigation operates at several scales. Global navigation establishes the product’s major areas; local navigation exposes what is available within one area; contextual links connect related information; utility navigation supports account, help, and settings; in-page navigation aids long or dense content.

Guide: Each should answer an orientation question without forcing people to memorise the structure. Breadcrumbs, headings, selected states, summaries, and clear page titles all contribute to wayfinding.

Guide: Design for entering from anywhere. Search, a shared link, a notification, and an external result may bypass the homepage. Give people enough context to understand where the page belongs, what else is nearby, and how to continue or recover.

Guide: Evaluate navigation with actual finding tasks, including wrong turns, lateral movement, returning after interruption, and moving between devices or channels.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Homepage assumption: pages work only when someone starts at the top of the hierarchy. Invisible location: a person cannot tell which section or state they are in. Menu overload: every destination is exposed at once without priority or grouping. Dead end: content has no next step, related path, or usable route back.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Someone opens a deep link from a search result, completes a small task, then cannot tell where they are, what else exists, or how to go back without losing progress.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Organisation and labels create the map. Navigation lets people move through it. lesson 40 adds search and information scent for people who arrive with a specific intent.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Deep-link into a site. Can you tell where you are, what is around you, and how to leave without starting over?

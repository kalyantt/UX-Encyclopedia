Learn at the Edges With Extreme Users and Co-design

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Learn at the Edges With Extreme Users and Co-design.

Guide: An “extreme user” is extreme only relative to a design assumption. Instead of recruiting unusual people for inspiration, identify assumptions likely to fail: stable attention, personal device ownership, precise movement, constant connectivity, standard documents, conventional address, trust in institutions, or predictable working hours.

Edge participation reveals model boundaries

Host: What changes when we look at edge participation reveals model boundaries?

Guide: Recruit people whose lived situations test those assumptions, including intersections. Their experiences reveal range, mechanisms, adaptations, and consequences; they do not estimate population prevalence or stand for everyone with a shared label.

Guide: Edge learning often benefits the core: captions help in noise, preserved progress helps interrupted users, clear status helps anxious first-timers. But “benefits everyone” should not erase the rights and expertise of disabled or marginalised people who motivated the change.

Co-design changes influence, not just attendance

Host: What changes when we look at co-design changes influence, not just attendance?

Guide: Participation can range from informing research, to consultation, collaboration, shared decisions, and community control. Name the level honestly. Co-design usually means affected people help frame, generate, critique, prototype, or select—not merely validate prepared options.

Guide: Before the work, define:

Guide: decisions open to influence and those constrained by law, safety, or organisational authority; participant roles and who remains absent; accessible formats, communication, support, time, location, and technology; compensation for lived expertise and associated expenses; consent, privacy, attribution, safeguarding, and withdrawal; how disagreement and power will be handled; how decisions and responses will be returned to participants.

Guide: Do not ask communities to solve structural constraints for free. Designers and organisations remain responsible for synthesis, implementation, evidence, and harm.

Transfer example: informal delivery addresses

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: informal delivery addresses?

Guide: Residents of informal settlements, couriers, and shared-phone customers may redefine a valid address through landmarks, contact timing, privacy, and recovery. If they only critique a conventional address form, the underlying service rule remains. Co-design can change the data model and failed-delivery policy; broader testing then examines feasibility and reach.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Inspiration extraction: difficult stories decorate a predetermined concept. Representative burden: one participant must speak for an entire community. Workshop access failure: the co-design activity itself excludes people. False power: choices are invited where the organisation has already decided.

Guide: Unpaid expertise: lived experience is treated as volunteering while professionals are paid. Special-mode containment: insights become a separate route and preserve the narrow default. No return: participants never learn what changed or why.

Select edge conditions from hidden assumptions

Host: What changes when we look at select edge conditions from hidden assumptions?

Guide: Research explores strategies and adaptations, not only breakdown. A screen-reader user may have an efficient heading and landmark strategy; a helper may maintain a parallel record because delegation is weak; a patient with intermittent connection may capture screenshots for continuity.

Guide: These practices can inspire broad improvements while also revealing needs that require a specific accommodation.

Guide: Co-design begins with a real decision boundary, accessible preparation, compensation, multiple ways to contribute, and a route for dissent. Participants influence criteria, concepts, prototypes, and governance where appropriate. The team does not ask them to design the interface from scratch, validate a predetermined solution, or carry responsibility for organisational choices.

Guide: Insights are transferred carefully. An edge case can expose a general mechanism but does not establish prevalence. Broader evaluation tests generalisation; specific access needs remain supported even when they do not benefit everyone. The outcome report shows what changed, what did not, who decided, and how participants will hear back.

Transfer example: shared financial access

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: shared financial access?

Guide: People managing money with a carer expose assumptions about one account, one device, one decision maker, and private notifications. Their experience can improve delegation and audit for many households while still requiring scoped permissions and safeguarding.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Participation redistributes authority and can expose conflict. Senior practitioners negotiate real decision space, budget compensation and access, protect dissent, and prevent urgency from reducing co-design to consultation. They recognise when community-led work or long-term partnership is more appropriate than a product workshop.

Use edge conditions to expose hidden assumptions

Host: What changes when we look at use edge conditions to expose hidden assumptions?

Guide: Co-design defines real influence, accessible participation, compensation, and decision follow-through. Participants are not asked to speak for an entire group or to solve structural problems alone. Findings inspire and challenge mechanisms, then are validated across the broader population.

Guide: The team records which improvement generalises and which remains a necessary specific accommodation.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a public-transit ticket machine. People with low vision, limited dexterity, unfamiliarity with the language, and high time pressure reveal different failure points. Invite them into problem framing and prototype evaluation, not just final approval.

Guide: Their experiences expose design demands, but no single participant should be treated as representative of an entire group.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Read a case study claiming co-design. Identify what participants actually controlled: framing, ideas, selection, implementation, or none. What evidence shows their contribution changed the decision?

Consumer Product Case: From Opportunity to Outcome

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Consumer Product Case: From Opportunity to Outcome. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Start with the progress people are trying to make: obtain the right groceries within a budget and time window while trusting that important household needs will be respected. “Reduce abandonment” is an organisational outcome. It becomes useful only when connected to the customer situation producing it.

Frame the opportunity around progress

Host: What changes when we look at frame the opportunity around progress?

Guide: Combine behavioural evidence with context. Funnel data can show when people leave; interviews, observation, support contacts, search queries, and fulfilment records can explain the conditions around that behaviour. Segment by meaningful circumstances—not just demographics.

Guide: A parent managing allergies, a shopper on a strict budget, and someone planning a recipe may interpret the same substitute very differently.

Guide: Write an opportunity statement that keeps the need and constraint visible: “People need to understand and control substitutions before purchase because an unsuitable replacement can waste money, break a meal plan, or create a health risk.” This gives the team a richer target than conversion.

Map the whole experience

Host: What changes when we look at map the whole experience?

Guide: Trace the journey from planning and search through selection, checkout, picking, delivery, review, and recovery. Add the backstage service: inventory freshness, fulfilment policy, picker tools, incentives, support authority, refund rules, and data returned to the catalogue.

Guide: At each stage ask:

Guide: What does the person believe is happening? What information do they need before making a commitment? What does the organisation know, and how reliable is it? Who makes the next decision? What happens when reality differs from the expected path?

Guide: This exposes causal distance. A checkout complaint may originate in inventory latency; a confusing picker choice may originate in weak catalogue relationships; a refund spike may originate in a policy that prioritises basket completion over suitability.

Generate alternatives at different system levels

Host: What changes when we look at generate alternatives at different system levels?

Guide: Do not move directly from insight to interface. Explore changes to information, interaction, policy, operations, incentives, and recovery.

Guide: Possibilities include showing inventory confidence earlier, letting customers define substitution rules by category, allowing “contact me” only for consequential items, giving pickers better equivalence information, or automatically refunding unsuitable substitutions without a support negotiation.

Guide: Compare concepts against customer value, operational feasibility, accessibility, privacy, business outcome, and failure consequence. A detailed preference system may offer control but create setup burden. A universal “no substitutions” choice may protect trust but reduce order completion.

Guide: The best direction may combine lightweight defaults with targeted control where consequences are higher.

Build the consumer target system

Host: What changes when we look at build the consumer target system?

Guide: The team models objects and states:

Guide: item, variant, ingredient/allergen claim, price and promotion; inventory observation with source and age; basket intent and substitution preference; proposed substitute, price difference, and equivalence reason; picker decision and unavailable state; customer approval where reachable; charge adjustment, delivery, rejection, refund, and correction.

Guide: It defines authority by consequence. A same-brand size change within a customer-set price rule may be permitted. An allergen-sensitive, infant, medical, religious, or explicitly “no substitute” item cannot be replaced merely because a recommendation is similar. “Contact me” needs an expiry and fallback so a picker is not blocked indefinitely.

Guide: The intervention combines:

Guide: 1. earlier inventory confidence and substitution expectation in search and basket; 2. lightweight rules by item/category with one-off override; 3. clear substitute comparison—quantity, unit price, ingredients, key differences, and reason; 4. a picker tool that exposes customer constraints and safe alternatives; 5.

Guide: stateful notification with limited decision window and accessible channel; 6. automatic price protection and low-friction refund/rejection; 7. feedback that corrects preference/catalogue relationships without treating every acceptance as universal truth.

Guide: This is not “a brighter checkout.” It changes information, interaction, fulfilment, incentives, data, and recovery together.

Prototype the riskiest decisions

Host: What changes when we look at prototype the riskiest decisions?

Guide: Choose fidelity based on uncertainty. Test wording and information order with sketches. Test the end-to-end decision with a clickable prototype containing unavailable items, price differences, allergies, and time pressure. Test backstage feasibility with fulfilment staff using realistic inventory cases. A polished happy path would conceal the very behaviour that matters.

Guide: Observe whether people understand the promise, can predict what will happen, notice consequential differences, recover from a mistake, and retain control. Include keyboard and assistive-technology use. Ask participants to explain the rule in their own words rather than relying on preference statements.

Define outcome and guardrails

Host: What changes when we look at define outcome and guardrails?

Guide: Use a balanced measurement model. The primary outcome might be successful orders people consider acceptable. Supporting measures can include substitution acceptance, repeat purchase, preference use, refund resolution, and time to complete.

Guide: Guardrails should cover unsuitable replacements, allergy-related incidents, price surprises, inaccessible completion, picker workload, support volume, and unequal outcomes across meaningful segments.

Guide: Launch in stages, monitor qualitative and operational signals, and define who can pause the change. A conversion lift does not justify a design that increases harmful substitutions or silently transfers more work to customers and staff.

Guide: The team distinguishes:

Guide: outcome: completed orders that customers judge usable and acceptable after delivery; diagnostics: inventory accuracy, preference coverage, response window, picker decision time, substitute reason, price adjustment, and refund path; guardrails: unsafe/allergen substitution, consent violation, price surprise, inaccessible decision, picker overload, support burden, lower fulfilment for budget-constrained shoppers, and repeated wrong substitute; countermetrics: checkout conversion beside post-delivery acceptance, refund, trust, and repeat willing purchase.

Guide: The initial rollout excludes consequential categories until ingredient and restriction data can support them. It includes representative low-stock regions, slower connections, assistive technology, and time-pressured fulfilment. A pause owner can disable automatic substitution while leaving explicit customer approval available.

Produce a defensible consumer case

Host: What changes when we look at produce a defensible consumer case?

Guide: The learner’s case should show:

Guide: opportunity and present workaround; behavioural pattern plus contextual mechanism; service journey and backstage incentives; object/state and authority model; alternatives and trade-offs; prototype of normal, unavailable, consequential, interrupted, and recovery states; accessibility and privacy decisions; measures, guardrails, rollout, and ownership; evidence limits and next decision.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Conversion frame: cart completion replaces acceptable household outcome. Preference overload: people must configure every item before receiving value. Silent delegation: accepting substitutions once becomes permission for unrelated future choices. Catalogue certainty: volatile inventory or incomplete ingredient data appears authoritative.

Guide: Picker externality: customer control creates unmeasured decision time and pressure backstage. High-frequency coercion: repeated prompts exploit time pressure or notification fatigue. Refund as safety: money is returned but health, time, meal, or trust harm remains. Easy-market pilot: affluent, well-stocked regions make service quality appear universal.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior product design is the ability to find the real leverage point without losing sight of the person’s outcome. The screen is often where a system decision becomes visible—not where it was created.

Guide: Consumer scale magnifies small defaults. Senior practitioners connect growth metrics to fulfilment truth, represent people who cannot absorb a wrong purchase, and understand how ranking, inventory, incentives, and repeated consent reshape behaviour. They remove friction selectively without removing informed choice.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a meal-delivery product. A team begins with abandoned repeat orders, investigates whether timing, choice, price, or unreliable delivery is responsible, tests several interventions, and measures completed meals and complaint patterns.

Guide: The case connects opportunity, research, interaction, service delivery, and outcome instead of presenting a polished screen as the solution.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Choose a consumer journey and trace one visible friction backward through information, policy, data, incentives, and operations. Propose three interventions at different system levels. For each, name the evidence needed, the customer outcome, one externalised burden, and one guardrail that could stop rollout.

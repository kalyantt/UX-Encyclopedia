Choosing the Right Method

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Choosing the Right Method.

Guide: Methods are instruments for different kinds of uncertainty. Generative research explores needs, context, behaviours, and language before a solution exists. Evaluative research tests whether people can understand and use a proposed design. Qualitative methods explain how and why; quantitative methods estimate scale, frequency, or relationships. No method is universally “best.”

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Choose based on the decision, stage, risk, access to participants, and evidence required. Interviews reveal reported experience and past behaviour; observation reveals action in context; usability tests reveal interaction breakdowns; surveys can compare patterns across larger samples; experiments estimate causal effects under controlled conditions.

Constraints change scope, not epistemology

Host: What changes when we look at constraints change scope, not epistemology?

Guide: If only one week is available, the team narrows the decision to whether the current “check again” state is dangerously misunderstood. It does not send a quick survey and pretend to have answered service redesign.

Guide: When direct observation is unsafe, researchers can use screen-sharing, artifact walkthroughs, retrospective reconstruction, and staff simulation while clearly naming what remains unseen.

Match method to claim

Host: What changes when we look at match method to claim?

Guide: Generative methods reveal context, goals, strategies, and unknown problems. Evaluative methods inspect a concept or implementation. Behavioural methods show action; attitudinal methods show reported belief or perception. Qualitative evidence explains mechanism and variation; quantitative evidence estimates pattern under defined measurement.

Guide: Choose according to decision, maturity, consequence, available artefact, population, environment, and access. Observation may answer workflow; usability testing interaction; survey distribution of a well-defined construct; experiment causal effect. No method is inherently rigorous outside a fit question and execution.

Guide: Combine methods when their limits are complementary, not to create an impressive list. State what each can and cannot support. Time pressure should narrow the decision or scope, not justify a method incapable of answering the question.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Triangulate when the stakes justify it. One method can produce a misleading picture because it captures only one part of reality. Combine methods to challenge a convenient explanation, not to manufacture certainty.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Method choice is a fit problem. Ask whether the team needs discovery or evaluation, explanation or estimation, a controlled comparison or natural context, individual behaviour or organisational process. Then consider the consequences of error, access to relevant people, maturity of the design, time available, and whether a method could cause harm or false confidence.

Guide: Methods can be combined deliberately. Observation may reveal work people do not report; interviews may explain its meaning; a prototype study can test a proposed response; telemetry can show whether the released pattern persists at scale. Each method has blind spots. A survey can quantify self-report but cannot prove actual behaviour.

Guide: An A/B test can estimate a local causal effect but cannot tell the team why a result occurred or whether an outcome is good for every affected group.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Fashionable method: the team runs a method because it is familiar, not because it answers the question. False equivalence: qualitative and quantitative evidence are treated as interchangeable. Unrealistic task: an evaluation measures prototype navigation rather than meaningful work. Single-method certainty: one data source is asked to answer questions outside its scope.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A survey can estimate how many customers report delivery anxiety, but observation and support cases reveal where tracking fails, while an experiment can test whether a specific update changes behaviour. One method cannot substitute for the others.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Assumptions tell us what is uncertain. This lesson matches uncertainty to evidence. lesson 22 asks whose behaviour that evidence must represent.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Match three product questions to a suitable method and explain why each method can answer that question.

Products are part of services

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Products are part of services. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: The repair booking page says a technician will arrive Tuesday. Tuesday passes without a visit. Was the page badly designed, or did the service behind it fail? The customer does not separate those questions. This lesson follows a product promise beyond the screen to the people, systems, and policies that must fulfill it.

What this moment reveals

Host: What changes when we look at what this moment reveals?

Guide: Most meaningful products sit inside a network of people, policies, operations, channels, and data. Service design looks across that network. It asks how a person moves through touchpoints and what backstage work makes the frontstage experience possible.

Guide: This shifts the design question from “How do we improve this screen?” to “What must work together for this person to get the outcome we promised?”

Guide: Services are co-created over time. The customer brings needs, choices, information, effort, and expectations. Staff, systems, partners, policies, and physical environments respond. The product interface is a boundary object: it can coordinate the relationship well or make the complexity invisible until it fails.

Follow the mechanism

Host: Can we follow the mechanism step by step?

Guide: Service map: trace “Book a home repair” across the visible journey and the work beneath it.

Guide: On the line: visible touchpoints—website, confirmation, live status, technician conversation, receipt.

Guide: Below the line: backstage elements—availability system, address data, technician dispatch, parts inventory, support policy.

Guide: Draw a red thread from an incorrect address in the booking flow to the failed visit. The lesson: a visible problem often begins somewhere else.

Guide: How to read a service map

Guide: A journey describes the person's steps, goals, questions, emotional highs and lows, and visible touchpoints. A service blueprint extends this by showing the line of interaction between customer and organisation, the frontstage actions visible to the person, and the backstage processes, systems, policies, and suppliers that enable them.

Guide: Use the map to locate dependencies and handoffs, not to create a decorative wall chart. Ask where information is created, copied, lost, or contradicted; who owns recovery when a promise breaks; which policy silently shapes the user experience; and whether staff have the information and authority needed to help.

Guide: Improvements should be tested across the full journey, especially around handovers and exceptions.

Guide: The difference between a journey and a service

Guide: A journey is seen through the customer's eyes. It is the sequence of events and touchpoints as they experience them, including what they are trying to do and how they feel at each point. A service is the system that makes that journey possible.

Guide: It includes visible interactions but also staffing, rules, data flows, technical integrations, capacity planning, procurement, and escalation paths.

Guide: This distinction helps a product designer know when to widen the room. If a screen repeatedly receives complaints, the best response may be an interface change. But if the screen is compensating for a late dispatch process, unclear policy, or missing staff authority, the cause belongs in the service.

Guide: Product teams become more credible when they can show the relationship rather than merely report that “the UI is broken.”

Guide: Design for the normal journey, then deliberately map the exceptions: no availability, missing data, changed mind, payment failure, late provider, disputed result, and escalation. The way a service handles these moments often determines whether the person trusts it afterwards.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Optimizing a single touchpoint while worsening the handoff to another team. Mapping only screens, not policies, staff, systems, and failure recovery. Assuming a status message fixes an operational problem. Forgetting that customers remember the broken promise, not the internal team boundary.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: When a digital fix does not improve the journey, inspect the policy, staff workflow, data, or handoff that the interface is exposing rather than causing.

Guide: Senior practitioners identify the owner and leverage point without claiming authority over every service function. They use blueprints to negotiate shared states, capacity, and recovery, then measure whether burden moved between channels. They treat a frontstage promise as an operational commitment with an accountable service owner.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A food-delivery app can present a perfect order tracker, but the experience depends on restaurant preparation, courier assignment, map accuracy, substitutions, refunds, and customer support. Interface design matters. So does the operational design that makes its promises true. When the service cannot meet its promise, clear and timely communication becomes part of the product.

Connect the lessons

Guide: lesson 4 made context part of the design. lesson 5 makes the system around the product visible, preparing the quality lens in lesson 6 and the later service-design module.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: When an app tells you “Your order is delayed,” ask: What backstage decision or constraint is this interface translating, and does the message help me act on it?

Scenarios and Stories

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Scenarios and Stories. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: A scenario is a concise story of a person pursuing a goal in context. It includes motivation, setting, constraints, actions, system response, and outcome. Scenarios make abstract requirements testable because they force a team to say who is doing what, why now, and what success looks like.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Use scenarios to explore before designing screens, to identify exceptions, and to test whether a proposed flow supports the actual activity. Keep them grounded in evidence; a scenario is not fiction for its own sake.

Build a coverage matrix

Host: What changes when we look at build a coverage matrix?

Guide: As evidence changes, details are updated with provenance. Fictional specificity never quietly becomes a requirement.

Use scenarios to expose interaction conditions

Host: What changes when we look at use scenarios to expose interaction conditions?

Guide: A scenario names actor, goal, context, trigger, prior state, constraints, sequence, and desired outcome. Begin without assuming the product response. Include environment, tools, other people, time, capability, and consequence where they change the work.

Guide: Write current, future, alternative, and failure scenarios. Use them to compare concepts and derive requirements. A story should reveal why each step matters and what information or authority is needed, not simply narrate clicks.

Guide: Test scenarios with participants and domain or operational experts. Mark evidence and assumption. Avoid one ideal story becoming a disguised specification; maintain variants for novices, experts, interrupted use, access needs, and recovery. Stories make systems discussable, but their selection also determines which realities the team sees.

Turn a story into design evidence

Host: What changes when we look at turn a story into design evidence?

Guide: Senior teams also distinguish three kinds of scenario variation. A context variation changes environment or capability, such as glare, screen-reader use, interruption, or shared-device access. A state variation changes what the service knows, such as an expired slot or incomplete clinical review.

Guide: A strategy variation changes how a person pursues the goal, such as deciding alone, delegating, or seeking reassurance. Mixing all three at once can make a failure impossible to diagnose, so test one variation deliberately before combining them in stress cases.

Guide: Finally, record the result as evidence: supported, partly supported, unsupported, or not yet known, with the observed failure and its consequence. The scenario portfolio becomes a coverage model for design review and evaluation.

Guide: It should change when research reveals a new strategy or when the service changes—not remain a fixed set of polished stories that merely confirms the current concept.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Use multiple scenarios to expose trade-offs: novice and expert, routine and urgent, successful and failed, private and shared. A single happy-path story creates false confidence.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Scenarios connect evidence to design by putting a goal under conditions. Write the actor, trigger, desired outcome, relevant constraints, information available, decisions, actions, system responses, exceptions, and completion state.

Guide: The details should be enough to expose what a design must support, but not so prescriptive that the scenario becomes a disguised screen specification.

Guide: Use a portfolio of scenarios: a primary success path, a novice or infrequent-use path, a time-pressured or interrupted path, an accessibility-relevant path, and a recovery or exception path. Run each proposed concept through the same scenarios. This reveals when a solution helps one context while creating new work or risk in another.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Happy-path fiction: a story assumes complete information and uninterrupted progress. Screen script: the scenario dictates interface controls instead of needs and consequences. Generic protagonist: the person has no relevant goal, constraint, or context. No outcome: the story ends at submission rather than whether progress was achieved.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: “User opens app and completes task” hides the reason, interruption, urgency, and consequence that determine whether an interaction is good. A scenario restores those conditions.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Write a six-sentence scenario for a real task, including context, constraint, action, outcome, and recovery need.

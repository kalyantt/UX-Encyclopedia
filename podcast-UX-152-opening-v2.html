Identity, Presence, and Collaboration

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Identity, Presence, and Collaboration.

Guide: Collaborative interaction requires people to understand identity, ownership, presence, permissions, activity, and change. A product should make it clear who can see, edit, approve, or be notified; what is happening now; and how conflicting work will be resolved.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Show shared state proportionately. Presence can be useful in real-time co-editing but distracting in focused work. History and attribution matter when accountability is high. Permissions need understandable consequences and recovery paths, especially when a change affects others.

Make people, authority, and change legible

Host: What changes when we look at make people, authority, and change legible?

Guide: Collaborative systems must represent who is present, who can act, what has changed, whose version is current, and how conflict is resolved. Presence indicators alone do not create collaboration. Identity, permissions, attribution, notification, locking or merging, and audit history form one behavioural model.

Transfer example: document editing

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: document editing?

Guide: Coloured cursors show presence, but collaboration also needs comment ownership, suggestion status, version history, access control, and recovery from conflicting edits. Social visibility must not outrun privacy or accountability.

Represent people and authority accurately

Host: What changes when we look at represent people and authority accurately?

Guide: Identity answers who performed or may perform an action; presence shows availability or activity; role and permission define authority. Do not infer identity from display name or presence from a green dot without explaining what the signal means. Support chosen names, duplicate names, localisation, privacy, and organisational context.

Guide: In collaborative work, show ownership, edits, comments, mentions, status, and handoffs. Distinguish viewing from editing and current presence from recent activity. Manage concurrency through locking, merging, conflict resolution, or version history appropriate to the object. Preserve attribution and recovery without turning every action into surveillance.

Guide: Permissions should follow real responsibilities and least necessary access. Explain unavailable actions and routes to request authority. Audit consequential changes with time, actor, object, and rationale while protecting sensitive activity.

Guide: Test social failure: mistaken identity, stale presence, simultaneous edits, removed access, impersonation, harassment, and notification overload. Collaboration quality depends on shared context, boundaries, and repair—not merely seeing avatars together.

Model authority and concurrency together

Host: What changes when we look at model authority and concurrency together?

Guide: Presence is evidence with limits. “Viewing” may mean a tab is open; “editing” may reflect a stale connection. The interface avoids implying attentive human review from a weak signal. Comments, assignments, and status changes show actor, role, time, and source. Privacy controls determine who may see presence at all.

Guide: Notifications correspond to responsibility, not every event. Audit history is human-readable while retaining diagnostic detail for authorised staff. Revoked delegation propagates across sessions and links. The team tests simultaneous work, role switching, expired access, offline change, impersonation risk, and recovery from conflict.

Transfer example: collaborative document

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: collaborative document?

Guide: Coloured cursors show activity, but safe collaboration also needs permissions, comment ownership, suggestion status, version history, conflict handling, and the ability to understand which version is authoritative.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Collaboration is not just multiplayer UI. It includes the policies, notification norms, ownership rules, and conflict-resolution paths that govern shared work.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Collaboration features need a shared model of people, roles, permissions, presence, ownership, changes, and conflict. Show who is viewing or editing, what is current, which action affects others, and how changes can be reviewed or reversed.

Guide: Choose real-time collaboration only when the task needs it; asynchronous handoff, notification, version history, and clear accountability may serve the work better.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Presence theatre: avatars imply live coordination without useful awareness or control. Permission surprise: people cannot predict who can see, edit, share, or delete work. Conflict loss: simultaneous changes overwrite one another without comparison or recovery. Ambiguous ownership: a shared object has no accountable person or clear next action.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Two people edit a shared document. One sees a cursor move, another sees a save conflict, and neither can tell who changed a key paragraph or whether their own draft is safe. Collaboration fails when shared state is ambiguous.

Guide: M09 has moved from reusable interaction patterns through action, creation, feedback, learning, and data. lesson 152 adds the social system around an interface. M10 applies the same system thinking to design systems used by whole organisations.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Inspect a collaborative tool. Identify who is present, who owns the object, what others can do, where history lives, and how a conflicting change is resolved.

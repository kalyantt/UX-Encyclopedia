Vocabulary, Synonyms, and Controlled Labels

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Vocabulary, Synonyms, and Controlled Labels. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Labels name choices, categories, actions, and states. Good labels use language people recognise, distinguish neighbouring concepts, and remain stable enough to support learning. Controlled vocabulary gives a system consistent preferred terms while preserving synonyms, aliases, and search cues for the language people actually use.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Do not label from an internal organisation chart. Study customer language, search queries, support conversations, and task context. Test labels in the place where they will be used: a heading can be clear in isolation but misleading beside competing options.

Govern the bridge between vocabularies

Host: What changes when we look at govern the bridge between vocabularies?

Guide: Products serve several vocabularies at once: the words people use, terms required by a domain, labels embedded in systems, and language used by search or analytics. A controlled vocabulary does not force everyone to use one phrase. It defines preferred concepts, accepted synonyms, disambiguation, scope, and ownership so different language can resolve to consistent meaning.

Guide: Candidate language comes from interviews, query logs, call transcripts, letters, analytics, and specialist review. The team tests labels in context through first-click, tree, and comprehension tasks. A term is not adopted “clear” because participants recognise it; they must predict the destination or consequence accurately. Confident misinterpretation is a rejection signal.

Guide: Vocabulary changes are versioned because they affect search mappings, content, components, training, support scripts, analytics, and sometimes legal meaning. The output includes synonym redirects and a migration plan. lesson 131 then uses these governed labels to establish wayfinding through the complex space.

Transfer example: security settings

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: security settings?

Guide: “Two-step verification,” “two-factor authentication,” “2FA,” and “login codes” may refer to related concepts. A vocabulary system can recognise the variants while distinguishing authentication method, recovery code, trusted device, and account recovery—preventing a friendly label from collapsing important security differences.

Build a vocabulary bridge

Host: What changes when we look at build a vocabulary bridge?

Guide: Collect terms from user research, search logs, support, domain experts, policy, analytics, and existing content. Identify preferred labels, synonyms, abbreviations, homonyms, broader and narrower terms, and context. Controlled vocabulary creates consistent indexing and display while preserving the language people actually use for retrieval.

Guide: Define every preferred term, scope, examples, exclusions, owner, and relationships. Use audience-facing labels where clarity permits; retain authoritative terms when they carry legal or technical meaning, then explain them. Map synonyms in search instead of forcing users to guess the official word.

Guide: Test sibling distinctness, destination prediction, scanning, translation, and assistive-technology pronunciation. Monitor zero results, reformulation, support language, and inconsistent authoring. A vocabulary changes with products and communities, so provide contribution, review, version, and deprecation.

Guide: Do not pursue uniformity at the expense of meaning. Different audiences may legitimately use different labels; the system should map them to shared concepts without pretending language is neutral.

Build a concept record that survives channels

Host: What changes when we look at build a concept record that survives channels?

Guide: Terms are tested in sentences and decisions, not as isolated preferences. Participants predict destinations, explain state, and choose an action. Staff and subject experts verify domain accuracy. Search logs and support language reveal new variants.

Guide: Confident wrong interpretation is more consequential than a term that takes a moment to learn, because it can produce the wrong action.

Guide: Change requires impact analysis. Renaming a state affects event data, component APIs, historical analytics, notifications, help, localisation, and staff language. The team records whether the concept changed or only its label, supports aliases and migration, and preserves historical meaning. Vocabulary governance is semantic versioning for the experience.

Transfer example: identity security

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: identity security?

Guide: Two-factor authentication, verification code, trusted device, recovery code, and account recovery are related but not synonyms. A controlled vocabulary can accept everyday queries while preventing an article or support agent from recommending the wrong security path.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Every renamed label creates a migration problem for existing users, documentation, analytics, and support. Treat terminology as product infrastructure, not decorative copy.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Treat vocabulary as a managed system. Define preferred terms, synonyms, deprecated labels, scope notes, translations, and ownership. Align labels across navigation, headings, search, metadata, analytics, support, and staff guidance so a person is not required to learn a new word at every touchpoint.

Guide: Use evidence from queries and support language to expose gaps between organisational and customer vocabulary.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Internal-term default: operational jargon becomes the primary label for a customer task. Synonym blindness: search and support fail because only one term is recognised. Uncontrolled rename: a new label breaks learned behaviour, analytics, and documentation. Ambiguous sibling: nearby labels overlap, so people cannot predict the difference.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A customer searches “refund,” but the help centre calls it “post-purchase adjustment.” The organisation has a precise internal term; the customer has a real question. Findability fails at the label.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

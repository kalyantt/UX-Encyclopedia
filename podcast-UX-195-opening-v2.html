Feedback Loops, Automation Bias, and Escalation

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Feedback Loops, Automation Bias, and Escalation.

Guide: A human–AI system does not merely observe a stable world. Its output changes what people notice and do, which changes recorded outcomes and future data:

Map the behavioural feedback loop

Host: What changes when we look at map the behavioural feedback loop?

Guide: input and context → model output → interface interpretation → human or automated action → service consequence → recorded outcome → training, evaluation, or policy data → future output

Guide: At each arrow, ask what is missing, selected, or transformed. Acceptance records agreement, not correctness. A closed case may mean resolution, abandonment, timeout, or pressure to clear the queue. An unflagged case receives less scrutiny and therefore produces less error evidence. A recommended provider gains demand, experience, and data, reinforcing its future ranking.

Guide: Add organisational incentives: handle-time targets, adoption goals, fear of override, staffing, vendor metrics, and leadership expectations. These determine whether people question the system and what the record comes to mean.

Automation bias and algorithm aversion

Host: What changes when we look at automation bias and algorithm aversion?

Guide: Automation bias is inappropriate reliance on automated output. It can involve commission—following a wrong suggestion—or omission—failing to notice something the system did not flag. It becomes more likely when:

Guide: workload and time pressure are high; output is confident, precise, ranked, or human-like; the task is complex and reviewer confidence is low; system success has been frequent; evidence and alternatives are hidden; accepting is easier or more rewarded than disagreeing; responsibility is ambiguous.

Guide: The opposite failure is algorithm aversion: after a visible error or loss of control, people reject useful assistance even when it outperforms the alternative. The design goal is neither maximum acceptance nor maximum scepticism. It is calibrated reliance appropriate to each case and consequence.

Make oversight structurally meaningful

Host: What changes when we look at make oversight structurally meaningful?

Guide: A human reviewer needs:

Guide: enough time and manageable volume; relevant expertise and independent evidence; clear knowledge of the system’s role and limitations; authority to disagree, delay, correct, or escalate; a path that does not impose punitive extra work; feedback about whether their intervention improved the outcome; protection from incentives that reward automatic agreement.

Guide: A mandatory approval click satisfies none of these by itself. Review quality should be tested under realistic load, interruption, fatigue, and ambiguity. Sample accepted cases as well as overrides; visible disagreement is only a fraction of possible error.

Guide: Design selective friction. Require comparison or verification for consequential, uncertain, novel, or irreversible cases. Avoid making every low-risk action laborious, which produces confirmation fatigue. Surface alternative options, decisive evidence, missing facts, and changed conditions—not a generic warning.

Design escalation as a service

Host: What changes when we look at design escalation as a service?

Guide: Escalation should be proportional to consequence and ambiguity:

Guide: Level 1 — correction: user or operator fixes input or preference directly. Level 2 — expert review: a qualified role resolves evidence or policy ambiguity within a stated time. Level 3 — incident response: potential systemic harm triggers containment, investigation, and affected-person support.

Guide: Level 4 — governance intervention: repeated or severe issues restrict scope, roll back, pause, or retire the system.

Guide: Show who owns the case, expected response, what happens while pending, evidence preserved, and whether the original action is paused. Escalation must reach authority capable of changing the outcome. Protect whistleblowing and dissent; a reviewer should not need to risk performance or status to challenge an automated decision.

Monitor second-order effects

Host: What changes when we look at monitor second-order effects?

Guide: Track acceptance and override alongside independent correctness, outcome, appeal, workload, queue age, response time, alert exposure, group distribution, source coverage, and model/data change. A sudden rise in agreement might mean the system improved—or that people stopped reviewing.

Guide: Investigate non-use and invisibility. Who never receives an intervention? Who abandons after a wrong output? Which clinics or patient groups generate too little data to evaluate? Monitoring only completed AI interactions selects for people whom the system already retained.

Guide: Use qualitative review to understand metric changes. Numbers can reveal that overrides fell; observation can reveal whether evidence improved or staff lost time to disagree.

Transfer example: content moderation

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: content moderation?

Guide: A classifier prioritises posts for moderator review. Flagged categories receive more scrutiny and therefore more discovered violations, reinforcing their apparent risk. Unflagged material supplies little evidence. Moderators under throughput targets accept labels, while creators face inconsistent appeal.

Guide: Meaningful oversight samples unflagged and accepted cases, separates moderator agreement from policy correctness, supports protected disagreement, and monitors which communities bear false positives and delays.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Agreement-as-truth: acceptance becomes a training label without independent outcome evidence. Rubber-stamp loop: workload and interface defaults turn human review into confirmation. Unflagged invisibility: cases outside model attention generate no discovered errors. Intervention contamination: changed outcomes are used as though no intervention occurred.

Guide: Popularity reinforcement: recommended options gain demand and data that justify further recommendation. Override penalty: disagreement requires more work or damages reviewer performance. Automatic retraining: behavioural exhaust enters the model without provenance, permission, or meaning review.

Guide: Escalation theatre: cases route to a queue without authority, capacity, deadline, or remedy.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior practitioners treat the product as an adaptive allocation system. They examine who gains attention, evidence, opportunity, and recourse as behaviour changes. They align staff metrics with decision quality, fund independent sampling and challenge, and give named leaders authority to narrow, pause, communicate, and remediate.

Guide: They also preserve epistemic independence. A system cannot validate itself solely with data produced by people acting on its outputs. Evaluation needs counterfactual thinking, external evidence, protected dissent, and sometimes randomised or blinded review.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a fraud-review tool. Reviewers begin accepting the model's “safe” label without checking evidence, and their decisions feed future training data. Monitor disagreement, overrides, missed harms, and incentives.

Guide: Make escalation easy and protect independent judgement, or the feedback loop can turn early mistakes into a seemingly stronger but less trustworthy system.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Inspect an AI recommendation at work or in a product. What makes accepting easier than questioning? What future data does your action create? Which cases receive no attention because they were not surfaced? Find the escalation route and ask whether the recipient has time, evidence, authority, and a deadline. If not, “human in the loop” is a label rather than a safeguard.

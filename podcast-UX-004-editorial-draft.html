Context changes design

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Context changes design. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: A train ticket is easy to retrieve at home. At the station, with a queue behind you, sun on the screen, and one hand holding luggage, the same task feels different. The interface has not changed. The conditions around it have. This lesson shows why context belongs inside the design problem.

What this moment reveals

Host: What changes when we look at what this moment reveals?

Guide: Context of use includes the person, their goals, tools, environment, social setting, time pressure, physical and cognitive conditions, and the consequences of error. It is not background detail. It determines what “good” means.

Guide: Design built only for an attentive person in ideal conditions will fail people who are rushed, tired, new to the domain, using assistive technology, interrupted, or making a high-stakes choice.

Guide: To understand context, examine more than device and location. Consider users' abilities, experience and confidence; the tasks they actually perform and why; physical conditions such as light, noise, movement and connectivity; social conditions such as shared devices and public viewing; and organisational or legal constraints.

Guide: Context is dynamic: people shift from novice to expert, calm to urgent, desktop to mobile, private to shared use.

Follow the mechanism

Host: Can we follow the mechanism step by step?

Guide: 1. At home on a laptop: time, stable connection, large screen. 2. On a platform: glare, noise, one-handed use, time pressure. 3. With a screen reader: sequential navigation, no reliance on visual position.

Guide: Under each, label the resulting requirements: save progress, strong contrast, plain language, large targets, predictable focus order, clear confirmation. The task stays constant; the design conditions do not.

Guide: Context is a design input, not an edge case

Guide: The common “average user” is a planning convenience, not a real person. Inclusive design starts from variation: temporary injury, limited dexterity, low vision, fatigue, different language proficiency, anxiety, or unfamiliarity with a domain can all change what is usable.

Guide: Designing flexibility into the primary flow often improves the experience for everyone: clear language helps busy experts; generous hit areas help walking users; visible status helps people with unreliable connections.

Guide: Do not guess context from demographic labels. Observe people in, or close to, the conditions where the activity happens. When direct observation is impossible, map assumptions explicitly and validate the riskiest ones first.

Guide: A context-of-use checklist

Guide: For every task, document six things. Who is attempting it, including prior knowledge and capabilities. What outcome they need, not merely the feature they touch. When it happens, including urgency, frequency, and interruptions. Where it happens, including light, noise, movement, privacy, and connectivity. With what devices, inputs, and assistive technologies.

Guide: What happens if it fails, including financial, social, physical, legal, and emotional consequences.

Guide: The last question is frequently neglected. A wrong tap in a photo editor and a wrong tap in a medication request are both “errors,” but they deserve very different prevention, confirmation, and recovery design. Context tells you which moments require speed, which require deliberate review, and which need a path to human help.

Guide: This is also why responsive design is insufficient as a definition of mobile UX. Rearranging elements for a small viewport may be necessary, but it does not answer whether someone can use the flow one-handed, recover after a call interrupts it, read it in glare, or complete it on a weak connection.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Treating “mobile” as merely a smaller desktop layout. Designing for a quiet, fast, fully attentive user by default. Adding accessibility after core interaction decisions are fixed. Ignoring the social context: shared devices, public screens, collaborators, caregivers, or supervisors.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: The context that is easiest to observe is rarely the one that creates the highest risk. Include stress, interruption, access needs, and operational dependency in the design question.

Guide: Senior practitioners decide which variation should be absorbed by a robust common design and which requires a genuinely different route. They fund research beyond convenient environments, connect situational limitation to permanent access needs, and challenge policies that make resilience impossible regardless of interface quality.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A parcel-delivery notification is not simply information. At work, the recipient may need a quick glance. At home, they may need to authorize a safe-place drop. In a shared household, privacy matters. If the recipient has limited connectivity, the message must remain useful without heavy media or an app download.

Guide: Good UX anticipates these conditions before an incident forces a redesign.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: lesson 3 introduced experience goals. lesson 4 explains why the same interface can support or frustrate those goals depending on real conditions. lesson 5 follows the journey beyond the interface.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: The next time a product asks for attention, ask: What does this design assume about my time, hands, vision, privacy, connection, and expertise?

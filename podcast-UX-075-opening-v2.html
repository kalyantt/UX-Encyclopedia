Accessible Interaction

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Accessible Interaction.

Guide: An online banking transfer looks straightforward with a mouse. Through a keyboard, focus disappears after recipient selection, and the final confirmation cannot be reached. Accessibility is not a property of the first screen. It is a contract that must hold through the whole task, including mistakes and recovery.

Accessible interaction is a complete contract

Host: What changes when we look at accessible interaction is a complete contract?

Guide: Every interactive element needs:

Guide: a perceivable and programmatic name; an accurate role, value, and state; operation through keyboard and relevant alternative input; visible, logical focus and reading order; understandable feedback for action, loading, error, and success; tolerance for timing, precision, repetition, and interruption; recovery that preserves context and entered information.

Guide: Native HTML controls provide established semantics and behaviour. Custom components inherit responsibility for keyboard patterns, focus, names, states, high contrast, touch, speech, screen readers, and every transition. Visual imitation is not semantic equivalence.

Guide: WCAG 2. 2 organises testable web requirements under perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust principles. Level AA includes all A and AA success criteria, not a score average. Conformance is a baseline for pages and processes; it cannot by itself prove an equitable, usable service.

Design the interaction path

Host: What changes when we look at design the interaction path?

Guide: Start with semantic structure: landmarks, headings, labels, instructions, relationships, and control types. Ensure all functionality works through a keyboard without traps. Focus order should follow meaning, not CSS position. Focus must remain visible and, under WCAG 2. 2 AA, not be entirely obscured by author-created content.

Guide: When context changes, manage focus deliberately. A modal receives focus at a meaningful start, contains it while active where the pattern requires, closes through predictable controls, and returns focus to the invoker. On route change or error, provide orientation rather than sending focus to an arbitrary top or disappearing node.

Guide: Do not require path-based gestures when a single-pointer alternative is possible. Provide alternatives to drag, hover, device motion, and complex gestures. WCAG 2. 2 AA’s Target Size (Minimum) uses a 24 by 24 CSS-pixel baseline with defined exceptions, but product guidance may choose larger targets based on context and consequence.

Guide: Allow enough time, pause moving content, avoid seizure-triggering flashes, support zoom and reflow, and do not block paste or password managers. Dynamic status needs programmatic communication without unexpectedly moving focus.

Transfer example: sortable data grid

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: sortable data grid?

Guide: A grid may be technically focusable yet unusable if arrow-key behaviour is undocumented, sort state is unannounced, virtualised rows disappear from the accessibility tree, or cell relationships are unclear. Sometimes a semantic table plus separate controls is more robust than an application-style grid. Pattern complexity must be justified by the task.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Focusable but unusable: name, state, consequence, or instructions remain unclear. Focus loss: dialogs, validation, or route changes destroy orientation. Gesture-only action: drag, hover, precision, or motion has no equivalent. Visual status only: colour, animation, or position carries essential meaning.

Guide: Component pass, journey fail: transitions, authentication, and third parties break access. Automation confidence: detectable rules stand in for manual and lived-experience evaluation.

Design the complete interaction contract

Host: What changes when we look at design the complete interaction contract?

Guide: Semantic HTML is the default because native controls carry established behavior. Custom components require an explicit keyboard and accessibility contract and should be used only when the interaction benefit justifies the maintenance risk.

Guide: Repeated “Choose” buttons include the item being chosen in their accessible names; disabled actions explain why; modal focus returns to the invoking control; and no essential action depends on drag, hover, or gesture alone.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Accessibility is a product-system quality. Put semantics, focus, target behaviour, error patterns, and test coverage into the design system; set procurement requirements for third parties; include accessibility defects in release governance; and give critical barriers stop-ship authority.

Guide: Maintain browser–assistive-technology support expectations and monitor regressions after release.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a banking transfer. A person using a keyboard needs to move through recipient selection, review, confirmation, and receipt without losing focus or context. Test the full task, including errors and recovery, with keyboard and assistive technology. An accessible landing page is not enough if the irreversible step cannot be understood or operated.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Navigate a service without a pointer from entry through error recovery. Record the first point where you cannot tell where focus is, what changed, or how to return. Trace whether the cause belongs to content, component, page, or service policy.

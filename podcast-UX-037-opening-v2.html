Information Architecture

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Information Architecture.

Guide: Information architecture organises, labels, navigates, and supports finding information in a context. It balances users’ vocabulary and goals, the nature and volume of content, and the systems that store and deliver it. Good IA makes a large information space understandable without forcing people to learn the organisation’s internal chart.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Start with content, users, and context. Identify dominant tasks, content relationships, changing states, governance, and findability needs. Organisation is a hypothesis to test through sorting, tree testing, search analysis, and observation.

How architecture changes what people can understand

Host: What changes when we look at how architecture changes what people can understand?

Guide: Information architecture operates below the menu. It defines the things in the domain, their attributes and relationships, the language used to describe them, and the routes by which people can retrieve them. Navigation, search, notifications, support tools, and AI retrieval are different views over this shared structure.

Guide: If the underlying model confuses a transfer request with a booked appointment, no amount of labelling polish can make every view truthful.

Guide: The team evaluates structure with difficult retrieval scenarios rather than asking whether categories look sensible. Tree tests examine whether people choose promising routes without screen design. Content-rich prototypes test whether context and cross-links support recovery. Search-log analysis reveals vocabulary and dead ends.

Guide: Operational walkthroughs check whether ownership and metadata can keep the structure accurate. No single method proves the architecture; together they expose different failure modes.

Transfer example: university information

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: university information?

Guide: Prospective students search by programme, qualification, cost, location, and application task, while a university may publish by faculty. A durable model retains faculty as ownership metadata but makes programmes, entry requirements, funding, and deadlines related objects. Navigation and search can then serve the student’s task without erasing institutional responsibility.

Model information as a system

Host: What changes when we look at model information as a system?

Guide: Identify content and object types, attributes, relationships, lifecycle, ownership, audience, and tasks. Separate the conceptual domain from current pages or menus; the same information may appear through navigation, search, recommendations, notifications, and support.

Guide: Balance users, content, and context. Research language, seeking behaviour, and mental models; audit volume, quality, metadata, and change; understand strategy, technology, governance, and constraints. IA choices determine what can be found, combined, reused, and maintained.

Guide: Prototype structures with representative content and difficult retrieval tasks. Evaluate findability, comprehension, orientation, and confidence. A tidy sitemap is not enough if labels conceal meaning or the content model cannot represent real relationships.

Model the information environment, not only the menu

Host: What changes when we look at model the information environment, not only the menu?

Guide: He models the domain before drawing navigation. A controlled vocabulary defines concepts such as referral, clinical review, ready to book, transfer, appointment, and preparation.

Guide: A content model records their attributes and relationships: an appointment belongs to a clinic and referral; preparation requirements may belong to clinic, specialty, or appointment; availability has a timestamp and source. This prevents one label from concealing several states and makes the same meaning reusable across search, messages, staff tools, and pages.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: IA is product strategy made navigable. A taxonomy decides what the organisation considers distinct, related, visible, and owned.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Information architecture is the shared structure behind content, navigation, search, labels, and governance. Begin by understanding the information ecosystem: content types, attributes, relationships, lifecycle, owners, source systems, volume, permissions, audience vocabulary, and tasks.

Guide: A useful IA can be expressed through content models, sitemaps, taxonomies, navigation models, search rules, and governance responsibilities—not only a menu.

Guide: Organisation always embodies a viewpoint. A product team may group information by internal department while users think by task, life event, object, or question. Many systems need multiple access paths because different people use different mental models. Test the architecture with representative tasks, content examples, tree tests, card sorts, search analysis, and observation.

Guide: Treat the structure as a maintained product capability: content and terminology evolve, so ungoverned IA decays.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Org-chart navigation: internal ownership becomes the primary way people must find help. Menu-first design: navigation is drawn before content relationships are understood. Single-path assumption: all people are forced to browse in the same way. No governance: labels, categories, and content drift until the structure loses meaning.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A site has useful content, but a person cannot tell whether to browse by task, topic, audience, or product. The problem is not missing content; it is missing structure.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Worked example A university site organised by departments forces prospective students to know internal ownership. Research reveals tasks around choosing, applying, funding, and preparing. The architecture retains departments as metadata and governance owners while creating task and programme routes for students. Search maps common language to official terms.

Guide: The redesign changes navigation, content model, and ownership together; moving menu items alone would preserve the same structural mismatch. Earlier modules explained goals, mental models, and evidence. IA applies them to content ecosystems. lesson 38 makes the organising principles and labels explicit.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Choose a content-heavy site and identify its primary organising principle: topic, task, audience, format, time, or something else.

Why Experiments Lie

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Why Experiments Lie.

Guide: Before launch, threats include a vague hypothesis, variants that differ in several mechanisms, underpowered design, wrong randomisation unit, predictable assignment, unrepresentative eligibility, and a primary metric weakly tied to value.

Audit the experiment as a causal system

Host: What changes when we look at audit the experiment as a causal system?

Guide: During execution, watch for:

Guide: sample-ratio mismatch (SRM), where observed assignment counts differ implausibly from configuration; treatment and control implementation differences beyond the intended change; inconsistent triggering or exposure; missing, duplicated, or variant-specific events; bots, internal traffic, and identity failures; interference, leakage, or users switching variants; outages, seasonality, campaigns, policy shifts, and concurrent experiments; novelty, learning, and carryover effects.

Guide: During analysis, threats include repeated peeking and stopping, outcome switching, unadjusted multiple comparisons, selective segments, inappropriate independence assumptions, outlier handling chosen after results, and confusing statistical with practical significance.

Guide: After analysis, teams can still overgeneralise, ignore concentrated harm, mistake a proxy for value, or tell a causal mechanism the experiment did not test.

Trust checks precede product interpretation

Host: What changes when we look at trust checks precede product interpretation?

Guide: Use A/A tests to evaluate platform false-positive behaviour, assignment, and metric variance. Monitor SRM and other invariants independently of outcome. Reconcile critical events against source-of-truth operational data. Compare logging schemas and event volumes by variant. Validate exposure and eligibility. Inspect time series for implementation changes and novelty.

Guide: Pre-specify primary metrics, guardrails, decision thresholds, duration, exclusions, and planned segments. Report confidence intervals, absolute effects, baselines, and practical significance. Preserve reproducible queries, code versions, experiment configuration, and anomalies.

Guide: Twyman’s Law offers a useful posture: any figure that looks unusually interesting or different is usually wrong. It is not a reason to dismiss discovery; it is a reason to investigate surprising wins as rigorously as losses.

Transfer example: checkout “lift”

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: checkout “lift”?

Guide: A new checkout logs completion on button press while control logs confirmed payment. It reports a 4% lift. Order reconciliation removes the gain. The experiment process did not fail by rejecting the result; its quality system succeeded. Without source reconciliation, statistical machinery would have made a logging defect persuasive.

A valid estimate can support a bad decision

Host: What changes when we look at a valid estimate can support a bad decision?

Guide: Even perfect randomisation cannot rescue a gameable OEC. A prompt can increase clicks by making the old path harder. Short-term activity may rise through novelty and later reverse. Aggregate improvement may hide harm to a smaller population. Interference may make treatment beneficial only while few users receive it.

Guide: The causal estimate must still be interpreted through product, ethical, and operational judgement.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: SRM dismissal: imbalance is treated as ordinary noise. Variant-specific telemetry: the treatment changes measurement as well as experience. Peeking and stopping: chance determines experiment duration. Multiplicity fishing: one favourable result is selected from many tests. Novelty permanence: initial curiosity is projected into durable behaviour.

Guide: Proxy win: target movement replaces user value. Clean-story pressure: inconvenient anomalies disappear from the decision record.

Audit the path from assignment to claim

Host: What changes when we look at audit the path from assignment to claim?

Guide: He checks whether patients can encounter both variants across devices, whether staff behavior changes because they know the experiment, whether clinic capacity creates spillovers, and whether late outcomes are censored. A lift in “transfer requests” may reflect easier accidental activation; a reduction in support contacts may hide people who gave up.

Guide: Guardrails and qualitative follow-up test these explanations.

Guide: The team keeps an experiment log with hypothesis, pre-specified analysis, implementation checks, deviations, null findings, and decision. Results are reported with effect size, uncertainty interval, population, duration, and limitations. A clean number is not automatically credible; credibility comes from the design and the inspectable chain behind it.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Create authority to invalidate results even when they support an executive preference. Separate experimentation platform health from team performance so people are rewarded for finding defects. High-stakes or surprising results deserve replication, longer follow-up, independent review, and mechanism evidence. An organisation that only celebrates wins will manufacture them.

Audit the path from assignment to conclusion

Host: What changes when we look at audit the path from assignment to conclusion?

Guide: A higher transfer-submission rate could reflect better comprehension, hidden consequences, or unsuitable requests. The team reviews appropriate resolution and safety guardrails, segments only with justified hypotheses, and reports uncertainty and practical effect.

Guide: Stopping rules and exclusions are defined in advance; surprising findings are treated as new hypotheses unless independently confirmed.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a donation page. A test appears to lift donations, but the variant ran mostly during a campaign while the control saw quieter days. Before trusting the result, examine assignment, exposure, sample ratio, instrumentation, repeated peeking, and whether one large gift drove the average. A clean-looking dashboard can still reflect a broken comparison.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: When you see an unusually large experiment lift, write five non-product explanations. What invariant, source-of-truth comparison, or follow-up would test each one?

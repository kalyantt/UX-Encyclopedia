Actions and Controls

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Actions and Controls. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Affordances describe what actions are possible; signifiers communicate where and how to act. Constraints prevent invalid actions; mappings connect a control to its effect. Together they make controls understandable before use.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Use labels, shape, placement, state, and feedback to make the action and consequence clear. Preserve familiar patterns where they support recognition. Provide shortcuts for frequent expert work without hiding the primary path. Disabled states should explain what is missing when the person can act to resolve it.

Build controls around an action contract

Host: What changes when we look at build controls around an action contract?

Guide: A control must communicate what can be done, the object it affects, whether it is available, the consequence, and the system response. Visual affordance contributes to this understanding, but so do labels, placement, convention, feedback, and prior experience.

Guide: A raised shape does not rescue an ambiguous action; a text link can be clear when its behaviour and destination are conventional.

Guide: All states form one contract: default, hover where relevant, focus, pressed, disabled or unavailable, loading, success, partial success, and failure. Keyboard activation, target size, focus order, repeated submission prevention, latency, and screen-reader name are tested with real content. A component is not complete if only its resting appearance exists.

Transfer example: deleting a workspace

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: deleting a workspace?

Guide: The visible “Delete” button is a small part of the interaction. The product must establish scope, affected collaborators, retained data, billing consequence, permissions, authentication, and recovery. A confirmation that merely repeats “Are you sure?” adds friction without improving the decision.

Specify the control contract

Host: What changes when we look at specify the control contract?

Guide: Every control communicates possible action through label, affordance, placement, state, and consequence. Use a button for an action, link for navigation, checkbox for independent choices, radio for one-of-many, and switch for an immediate binary state when that model is true. Visual similarity must not erase semantic difference.

Guide: Define default, hover, focus, active, disabled, loading, success, and error. Prefer explaining why an action is unavailable over an unexplained disabled state. Name actions with the real verb and object, especially before irreversible effects. Ensure target size, keyboard operation, and assistive-technology state.

Guide: Test the action inside the task; a locally clear control can remain globally ambiguous.

Bind every control to a clear state transition

Host: What changes when we look at bind every control to a clear state transition?

Guide: He checks affordance, signifier, mapping, feedback, target size, focus order, keyboard operation, voice label, and behavior at zoom. Icon-only actions are used only when their meaning is established and the accessible name is explicit. Repeated controls identify their target in context; a screen reader should not encounter six indistinguishable “Select” buttons.

Guide: Control quality is tested across latency and failure. Multiple activation must not create duplicate bookings. Progress feedback must not imply completion before the authoritative service confirms it. If an action succeeds backstage after the interface times out, recovery should reveal the durable state. The component is complete only when the full transition is designed.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Control design is policy. What the system lets people do, prevents them doing, or makes difficult expresses trust, risk appetite, and accountability.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Controls should let people answer three questions before acting: What can I do? What will happen if I do it? Can I change or recover from it? Signifiers can be visual, textual, spatial, or behavioural; labels often carry the most reliable meaning.

Guide: Mapping matters especially when the action is distant from its effect, as in a toolbar affecting a selected object or a toggle changing a setting elsewhere.

Guide: Constraints can prevent unsafe or impossible actions, but unexplained disabled controls create confusion. When someone can resolve the condition, say what is required and provide a route to do it. When a control acts on an object, keep the object and consequence visible.

Guide: Provide keyboard operation, focus indication, touch targets, and state feedback as core behaviour rather than edge polish.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Mystery icon: appearance implies an action without a clear label or accessible name. Weak mapping: a control’s effect is remote, delayed, or visually ambiguous. Disabled dead end: the product blocks action without explaining the prerequisite. Shortcut-only power: frequent work is efficient only for people who discover hidden commands.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A control looks like a heading until someone hovers it. Another looks destructive but opens a preview. People should not have to experiment to learn the consequence of a control.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Interaction type sets the overall mode. Controls make that mode actionable. lesson 45 applies this to the highest-friction interaction pattern: forms.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Find a control whose behaviour is unclear before use. What signifier, label, or feedback would reduce the guesswork?

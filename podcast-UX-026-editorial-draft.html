Outcomes Over Outputs

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Outcomes Over Outputs. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Outputs are features, screens, releases, and deliverables. Outcomes are changes in behaviour, capability, experience, or business value. Teams need outputs, but outcomes prevent shipping from becoming the definition of success.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Frame outcomes around a person and an observable change. Then identify leading evidence, guardrails, and the assumptions connecting a solution to the outcome. A feature can launch perfectly and still fail to create value; an outcome lens creates permission to change or stop the feature when evidence demands it.

Write an outcome as observable change

Host: What changes when we look at write an outcome as observable change?

Guide: The team builds a behaviour chain from comparison to informed selection, valid request, acknowledged review, appropriate appointment, and prepared attendance. Measures at each link diagnose failure, while guardrails cover safety, staff workload, access, and unresolved cases.

Guide: Shipping remains necessary, but it becomes one intervention whose contribution is tested rather than the definition of success.

Worked contrast: the metric can improve while the service worsens

Host: What changes when we look at worked contrast: the metric can improve while the service worsens?

Guide: Imagine the comparison doubles digital booking. A shallow outcome review declares success. A system review finds that the interface defaults to distant clinics with earlier dates, cancellation rises, staff manually correct unsuitable transfers, and patients without digital access wait longer because capacity moved.

Guide: The output worked and the proxy improved; the intended outcome did not.

Write an outcome with a causal model

Host: What changes when we look at write an outcome with a causal model?

Guide: Specify whose state or behaviour should change, from what baseline, in which context, by when, and why that represents value. Separate customer outcome, business outcome, product behaviour, and team output. “Launch recommendations” is output; “people make a confident suitable choice” is closer to outcome.

Guide: Map assumptions from intervention to behaviour and result. Add guardrails for trust, accessibility, safety, cost, and unintended work. Choose leading evidence without allowing an easy proxy to replace the desired change.

Guide: Review outcomes through segments and longer horizons. A local conversion gain may conflict with retention or staff burden. Outcome orientation is not metric obedience; it is explicit reasoning about value and consequence.

Build a behaviour chain to the outcome

Host: What changes when we look at build a behaviour chain to the outcome?

Guide: Measures distinguish leading behaviour, service outcome, and longer-term value. Guardrails cover safety, original-appointment loss, unequal delay, staff workload, and support. If request submission rises while suitable appointments do not, the team investigates the broken link rather than declaring success.

Guide: Outcome language also clarifies ownership. Product teams can influence comprehension and request quality; clinics and operations control other parts. A whole-service owner coordinates the shared result so internal boundaries do not shrink the customer promise.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Outcome framing exposes conflicting incentives. A short-term engagement lift may damage trust or completion quality. Define the user benefit and guardrails before optimising the visible number.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: An outcome has a subject, a change, a context, and a reason it matters. “Improve activation” is too broad until the team can say which people should reach which meaningful behaviour, under what conditions, and why that signifies value.

Guide: Connect outcome measures to a theory of change: the opportunity addressed, the intervention, the expected mechanism, and possible negative effects. This makes it possible to learn when the mechanism fails rather than simply declaring a feature unsuccessful.

Guide: Use a balanced measure set. Leading indicators help teams see whether a path is forming; outcome measures show whether value persists; guardrails reveal harm, exclusion, support burden, privacy cost, or declining trust. Avoid using a metric as a target without inspecting what behaviour it incentivises.

Guide: An outcome is not merely a business number; it should represent a better state for the people and system involved.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Output theatre: releases are celebrated while their intended change is never measured. Metric proxy: a convenient count replaces the value it was meant to represent. No guardrails: a local improvement hides harm or workload shifted elsewhere. Frozen outcome: a goal remains relevant only to an old strategy or context.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Releasing reminders is an output. Fewer missed appointments may be an outcome, but only if reminders reach people, are understood, and do not increase privacy harm or cancellation barriers.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: Discovery is a habit because outcomes must be revisited. lesson 27 teaches how recurring conversations reveal the past behaviour behind those outcomes.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Convert “launch feature X” into a measurable user outcome. What behaviour would show it worked?

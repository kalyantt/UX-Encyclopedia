Prototype Fidelity, Representation, and Behaviour

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Prototype Fidelity, Representation, and Behaviour. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: lesson 61 established that prototypes answer questions. Advanced practice controls fidelity dimension by dimension. Treat it as a vector, not a ladder from paper to “high fidelity”:

Fidelity is a vector

Host: What changes when we look at fidelity is a vector?

Guide: visual and brand detail; content specificity; data realism and variability; interaction and state behaviour; temporal realism, including latency and expiry; technical implementation and performance; breadth across journeys and edge cases; social, operational, and environmental realism.

Guide: Increase only the dimensions necessary for a credible response. A low-visual prototype may need high content and operational fidelity. A coded component may need real keyboard behaviour and latency while the surrounding journey remains schematic.

Guide: Representation determines what participants must imagine. Paper supports rapid structural comparison but cannot convincingly model focus, motion, timing, or assistive technology. Clickable prototypes model sequence but often fake data and system state. Wizard-of-Oz prototypes let a human secretly perform an unbuilt capability.

Guide: Concierge prototypes make the human service explicit. Coded slices test technical behaviour. Role-play and service rehearsal expose handoffs and operational load.

Build a fidelity plan

Host: What changes when we look at build a fidelity plan?

Guide: Write the claim and consequence first. Then make a table with fidelity dimensions, required level, why it matters, how it will be simulated, and what remains unknowable. Include entry, decision, response, delay, exception, recovery, and completion.

Guide: Define the simulation contract for researchers and observers:

Guide: what is real, generated, scripted, or manually operated; which participant actions have genuine consequences; how variable outputs and failures are selected; when a facilitator may intervene; which claims are explicitly out of scope.

Guide: Pilot for credibility. If participants know a delay is fake, they may wait differently. If a health scenario uses generic text, it may not evoke the comprehension burden of real terminology. Fidelity is sufficient when the remaining artificiality does not invalidate the decision—not when the artifact looks finished.

Transfer example: an AI support assistant

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: an ai support assistant?

Guide: An AI prototype should include variable latency, missing sources, uncertainty, one plausible wrong answer, and human escalation. A facilitator can supply outputs behind the scenes. This can test whether agents notice uncertainty, verify claims, and recover. It cannot prove model accuracy, operating cost, security, or production performance.

Guide: One ideal scripted answer would test presentation while hiding the defining behaviour of the system.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Visual-fidelity bias: polish creates false confidence and narrows critique. Behavioural emptiness: screens work but data, delay, and failure are idealised. Unstated Wizard: participants and observers misunderstand what is automated. Prototype leakage: shortcuts become production architecture. Wrong breadth: a broad shallow flow or narrow detailed slice misses the actual risk.

Guide: Synthetic calm: low-stakes simulation supports claims about high-stakes behaviour.

Build a fidelity profile, not a low-to-high ladder

Host: What changes when we look at build a fidelity profile, not a low-to-high ladder?

Guide: Every prototype has a declaration: decision, hypothesis, realistic elements, simulated elements, omitted elements, participant assumptions, success evidence, and claims it cannot support. A human concierge can test whether people value guided transfer and whether the service steps make sense; it cannot prove an automated model can make the same judgment or that staffing scales.

Guide: Findings choose the next representation. A comprehension failure returns to content and state. A handoff failure triggers service rehearsal. A data uncertainty requires audit or technical spike. A safe, coherent concept may proceed to a limited live pilot with monitoring and rollback. Progress is measured by retired uncertainty, not visual finish.

Transfer example: voice assistant

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: voice assistant?

Guide: A scripted human behind a voice prototype can test conversational flow and user expectation. It cannot validate speech recognition, latency, privacy, error distribution, or model safety. The prototype report must keep those claims separate.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior designers negotiate prototype truthfulness. They involve engineering, operations, content, accessibility, security, and domain specialists in deciding which conditions must be real. They budget separately for exploratory code and production hardening. For AI and other adaptive systems, they prototype distributions and failure classes, not a curated output.

Guide: Every review should end with two statements: what evidence increased confidence, and what the representation could not reveal. This prevents realism from leaking into claims about demand, feasibility, safety, accessibility, or field outcomes.

Tune each dimension of fidelity to the question

Host: What changes when we look at tune each dimension of fidelity to the question?

Guide: The team labels what is real, simulated, omitted, and impossible to infer. A smooth human-operated prototype may prove desirability while hiding automation and scale risk. Findings choose the next representation: technical spike, service rehearsal, content test, or limited live pilot.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a grocery substitution. A paper sketch can test whether shoppers understand the choice; a clickable prototype can test navigation; a service rehearsal may be needed to test messages from staff. Choose the least fidelity that preserves the behaviour under study. A convincing visual mockup can mislead if the operational timing is fictional.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Inspect a product demo. List which dimensions are genuinely realistic and which are staged. What important conclusion becomes unsafe if viewers treat all dimensions as equally faithful?

Research Starts With a Decision

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Research Starts With a Decision.

Guide: Research is valuable when it reduces uncertainty around a decision. Start with the decision to be made, the consequences of being wrong, what is already known, and the smallest evidence that could materially change the direction. Then form a research question that can be answered with observable evidence.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Avoid vague goals such as “understand users.” They invite broad activity without a decision owner or a definition of useful evidence. A good question specifies the behaviour, context, and uncertainty: “What information do first-time buyers need before selecting delivery, and where do they look for it?”

Decision table

Host: What changes when we look at decision table?

Guide: Before recruitment, the team records likely findings and actions. If most failures are transport-related and alternate clinics have available capacity, compare-clinic support becomes credible. If clinical rules determine eligibility, self-service transfer is unsafe. If patients merely cannot interpret filters, repair the current choice architecture.

Guide: This pre-commitment prevents an interesting interview quote from becoming a convenient post-hoc justification.

Guide: Research does not guarantee certainty. Its job is to change the quality of a decision: clarify which option fits, expose a missing option, reduce scope, or show that the team must not decide yet. If no plausible result can change action, the activity is documentation or evaluation, not discovery.

Work backwards from the decision

Host: What changes when we look at work backwards from the decision?

Guide: Each method earns its place by changing a defined uncertainty. Interviews explore past attempts and mental models; operational observation exposes handoffs; log analysis estimates frequency and delay; a prototype tests interpretation. The plan also states what evidence would stop the concept or narrow its scope.

Guide: Research has influence because the decision and evidence standard were agreed before results arrived.

Work backward from action

Host: What changes when we look at work backward from action?

Guide: Name the decision owner, choice, timing, alternatives, consequence, and current uncertainty. Ask what evidence would change the decision and what will happen if results are ambiguous. This prevents broad goals such as “understand users” from producing interesting but unusable findings.

Guide: Translate the decision into research questions about behaviour, context, need, comprehension, feasibility, or outcome. Select participants and methods after the question. Define the claim each method can support and the evidence threshold proportionate to risk.

Guide: Plan how findings enter the decision: debrief, artefact, review, owner, and follow-up. If scope is already fixed and no result can alter it, be honest that the work is evaluation or risk documentation rather than discovery.

Set an evidence threshold before collecting it

Host: What changes when we look at set an evidence threshold before collecting it?

Guide: The threshold scales with reversibility and harm. A low-risk copy adjustment may proceed with quick evaluative evidence and monitoring; a change affecting clinical routing, privacy, or access requires domain review, broader representation, and stronger operational proof. Time pressure is recorded as a constraint, not disguised as confidence.

Guide: When research ends, the decision log states what changed, what remains uncertain, and who owns the next step. If no plausible finding could have changed the plan, the work was not decision-led research. This discipline protects participants' time and keeps evidence from becoming decorative validation.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Research scope is a risk decision. Spend more effort where the decision is consequential, irreversible, expensive, or weakly evidenced. Do not use research as theatre after the decision has already been made.

Look more closely

Host: What should we look at more closely?

Guide: Frame research as a decision brief. Name the decision owner, deadline, possible choices, cost of a wrong choice, existing evidence, and the signal that would change the choice. This prevents “research” from becoming a request for reassurance after a commitment has already been made.

Guide: A decision can be strategic, such as whether an opportunity is real, or operational, such as whether an error message enables recovery.

Guide: Separate learning questions from solution preferences. “Will people like our new dashboard?” bundles desirability, comprehension, utility, and political hope into one question. Break it into answerable uncertainties: what job are people trying to do, what information do they use, can they interpret the proposed view, and does it improve a meaningful outcome?

Guide: Choose the smallest responsible study that can move the decision, then plan how the finding will be used.

Guide: Failure modes to watch

Guide: Research theatre: evidence is requested after the decision is irreversible. Question without action: no one can state what a result would change. Method-first framing: a team chooses interviews or a survey before naming uncertainty. Over-broad inquiry: “understand users” produces material that cannot guide a decision.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Before redesigning checkout, ask whether the decision concerns guest access, address burden, payment trust, or fulfilment eligibility. Each demands different evidence; a generic usability test cannot answer them all.

Connect the lessons

Host: How does this connect to what we have already learned?

Guide: lesson 18 taught requirements as evidence. This lesson asks how to create that evidence responsibly. lesson 20 makes hidden assumptions visible before they harden into design.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Turn “users hate this” into a decision, then write the research question that could change that decision.

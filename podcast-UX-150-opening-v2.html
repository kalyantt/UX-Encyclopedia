Onboarding and Progressive Disclosure

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Host: Onboarding and Progressive Disclosure.

Guide: Onboarding helps people form a usable model, take a first meaningful action, and know where to get help later. Progressive disclosure reveals complexity when it becomes relevant, without making critical options impossible to find. Both are about timing and context, not hiding features for their own sake.

Why it happens

Host: Why does that happen?

Guide: Start from the person’s first job. Show the smallest amount of information that enables a safe next step, then use the product itself to teach through labels, defaults, examples, feedback, and recovery. Let people skip, revisit, or ask for help. A guided tour is useful only when it changes what someone can do afterward.

Teach at the moment knowledge becomes useful

Host: What changes when we look at teach at the moment knowledge becomes useful?

Guide: Onboarding should help a person reach initial value and form an accurate model, while progressive disclosure manages complexity by revealing detail when it becomes relevant. Neither means hiding consequential information. A tour shown before the user has a goal is easily forgotten; a condition revealed after commitment is deception.

Guide: The team layers information by decision timing. Clinic cards show comparison essentials; details reveal access evidence and conditions; review surfaces every material consequence before submission. Returning users retain shortcuts and do not repeat orientation, but changed policy or capability is introduced contextually with acknowledgement where needed.

Transfer example: analytics software

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: analytics software?

Guide: A blank dashboard tour teaches labels without purpose. Letting a user connect one data source and answer a real question creates situated learning; advanced segmentation can appear after the underlying measure is understood.

Design onboarding around first value

Host: What changes when we look at design onboarding around first value?

Guide: Identify what a person must understand or configure before meaningful progress and what can be learned in context. Remove unnecessary setup, prefill safely, let people skip optional education, and retain a path back. A tour that explains every control before need creates recognition without understanding.

Guide: Use progressive disclosure to defer complexity, not conceal consequence. Reveal advanced options when the person’s goal, role, or state makes them relevant. Keep critical status, price, privacy, limitations, and irreversible effects visible before commitment. Support experts who need direct access.

Guide: Measure activation as successful value, not tour completion. Examine time to first meaningful outcome, errors, help, abandonment, later discovery, and retention. Segment novices, returning users, roles, and accessibility needs. Re-onboard when capability materially changes, but explain the change rather than replaying generic education.

Guide: Test interrupted and resumed journeys. Onboarding should create a usable mental model and confidence to continue, not dependence on remembered instructions.

Build a learning path around real progress

Host: What changes when we look at build a learning path around real progress?

Guide: Progressive disclosure separates secondary detail from material consequence. Long clinical and policy explanations can be layered, but eligibility, uncertainty, data sharing, effect on the existing appointment, and the meaning of the request must appear before commitment.

Guide: Disclosure responds to the task and state; it does not conceal an unfavourable condition until the organisation has captured the action.

Guide: The team measures time to meaningful progress, correct mental model, setup abandonment, later help and error, repeated-use efficiency, and recovery after interruption. It tests people arriving from different entry points and with different prior knowledge.

Guide: Changes in policy or capability receive contextual re-onboarding, with severity and acknowledgement proportionate to their effect.

Transfer example: analytics workspace

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: analytics workspace?

Guide: A generic product tour points at navigation before any data exists. A stronger sequence helps the user connect one source, validates what has been imported, and answers one real question. Advanced segmentation appears when the basic measure and denominator are understood.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Progressive disclosure is an information-risk decision. Senior practitioners specify which facts can safely wait, which must precede commitment, and how returning users learn changed policy without being trapped in repetitive onboarding.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: A new user opens a project tool and is shown twelve tips, a video, three setup modals, and an empty dashboard. The product explains everything before the person has a reason to care about any of it.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Review a first-use flow. Mark what is taught before it has meaning, what appears at the right moment, and what consequential condition is disclosed only after action.

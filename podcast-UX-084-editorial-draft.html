Data, Drift, and Governance

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Data, Drift, and Governance. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: AI quality depends on a lifecycle: purpose definition, data collection and rights, preparation, development, evaluation, deployment, monitoring, feedback, change, incident response, and retirement. A model file is only one component.

Guide: Retrieval sources, prompts, thresholds, tools, permissions, interface cues, policies, staff practices, vendors, and downstream decisions all shape the experience.

A deployed model is a living service

Host: What changes when we look at a deployed model is a living service?

Guide: Governance assigns decision rights and evidence across that lifecycle. It answers:

Guide: who owns the intended use and affected outcomes; who may change models, prompts, data, sources, permissions, and authority; what evidence is required before and after a change; which signals trigger review, restriction, rollback, communication, or retirement; who can pause the system immediately; how affected people obtain explanation, correction, and remedy; what record supports investigation and accountability.

Guide: A dashboard without an owner and intervention authority is observation, not governance.

Understand different kinds of drift

Host: What changes when we look at understand different kinds of drift?

Guide: “Drift” is not one condition:

Guide: Data or covariate drift: the distribution of inputs changes—for example, new phrases or different patient populations. Label or outcome shift: the frequency of relevant outcomes changes, such as clinic availability or referral demand. Concept drift: the relationship between input and correct output changes; a policy or clinical pathway gives an old phrase a new implication.

Guide: Source drift: retrieved content, schemas, ownership, freshness, or authority changes. Behavioural drift: people adapt to the system, intentionally or unintentionally changing inputs and decisions. Product drift: interface, permissions, action scope, or surrounding workflow changes while the model remains the same.

Guide: Value drift: the metric still improves, but the outcome no longer represents what users or the service need.

Guide: Not every distribution change is harmful, and a stable distribution does not prove safety. Monitoring should follow the assumptions and consequences that justified deployment, not simply calculate technical distance from training data.

Create a living system record

Host: What changes when we look at create a living system record?

Guide: Maintain a registry entry that is usable by product, design, engineering, operations, risk, and affected-support teams. Include:

Guide: intended and prohibited uses; affected people and accountable owner; model, prompt, threshold, and tool versions; training, evaluation, retrieval, and operational data sources; provenance, permissions, retention, and known representation gaps; delegation contract and action authority; evaluation results, limitations, thresholds, and baselines; dependencies, vendors, fallback, and rollback; monitoring signals, cadence, reviewers, and incident severity; user feedback, recourse, and communication routes; release history, material changes, open risks, and retirement plan.

Guide: Version changes that can alter behaviour even when the visual interface does not: a foundation-model update, prompt edit, ranking weight, clinic-data schema, safety policy, tool permission, or new memory feature. Define in advance which changes require regression evaluation, usability research, risk review, staged release, or notice to people.

Monitor leading and lagging signals

Host: What changes when we look at monitor leading and lagging signals?

Guide: Leading signals reveal deteriorating conditions before final harm: schema failures, source age, unfamiliar inputs, calibration change, abstention, latency, permission errors, queue growth, or override patterns. Lagging signals reveal realised outcomes: complaints, inappropriate referrals, access failures, financial loss, harm, appeal, or inequity.

Guide: Combine them. A leading alert without outcome context creates noise; a lagging measure alone waits for people to be harmed. Examine segments and intersections relevant to the use, while protecting privacy and avoiding unstable conclusions from tiny samples.

Guide: User reports are evidence, not the monitoring system. If affected people must repeatedly prove degradation before an owner acts, governance has failed.

Change, incident, rollback, and retirement

Host: What changes when we look at change, incident, rollback, and retirement?

Guide: A safe change process states what changed, why, expected effect, evaluation performed, exposure plan, owner, monitoring window, and rollback criterion. Use staged release when risk warrants it. Preserve enough version and event evidence to reconstruct consequential behaviour without collecting unnecessary sensitive content.

Guide: Incident response needs severity definitions, immediate containment, technical and service investigation, affected-person communication, remedy, regulatory or contractual escalation where applicable, and learning that changes the system. Avoid quietly fixing the model while leaving harmed people without correction.

Guide: Retirement is a designed state. Decide how active cases continue, what records remain, how permissions and integrations are revoked, what people are told, and which non-AI or human path replaces the feature. A system should not survive solely because no committee owns the decision to stop it.

Transfer example: hiring recommendation

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: hiring recommendation?

Guide: A hiring model may remain technically stable while job roles, labour markets, application behaviour, or organisational priorities change. Recruiters may adapt submissions to the model, creating a feedback loop.

Guide: Governance must monitor selection and downstream outcomes, reviewer behaviour, group-level effects, role changes, appeals, and whether the original use remains legitimate—not simply feature distributions.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Launch and forget: evaluation ends when the release is approved. Unowned dashboard: signals exist but nobody can restrict, pause, or remedy. Silent change: model, prompt, source, permission, or authority changes without re-evaluation. Accuracy tunnel vision: source freshness, workflow, complaints, and downstream outcomes are ignored.

Guide: Feedback-loop blindness: people adapt to the system and reinforce its errors or incentives. Automatic learning: unreviewed corrections or behaviour enter future models as assumed truth. Compliance archive: documentation is created for review but not used in product decisions.

Guide: No retirement path: integrations and active cases make an unsafe feature practically impossible to remove.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior practitioners make governance executable. They secure named owners, budgets, review cadence, incident capacity, independent challenge, and pause authority. They translate external frameworks into the actual product lifecycle while keeping responsibility with the organisation—not the framework, vendor, or user.

Guide: They also ask second-order questions: Does monitoring create privacy risk? Does a fairness intervention reduce access elsewhere? Does fallback overload staff? Does showing explanations change behaviour and future data? Governance is continuous judgement under changing evidence, not proof that uncertainty has disappeared.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: an inventory predictor. A model trained on last year's sales begins to miss demand after a new fulfilment policy changes ordering behaviour. Monitor input quality, subgroup performance, overrides, and downstream waste. Give an owner authority to pause or revert the feature. Drift is an operational and governance problem, not merely a model statistic.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Choose a live AI feature and imagine it one year from now. What source, population, policy, workflow, incentive, or capability could have changed without altering the screen? Look for a visible update date, model notice, limitation, feedback route, or accountable owner. If none exists, ask whether the product presents a changing service as though it were a static interface.

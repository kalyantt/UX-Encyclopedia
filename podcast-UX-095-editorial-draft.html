Evaluate Value, Risk, and Harm Together

This recording is an editorial draft adapted from the revised lesson. It has not completed the final dialogue and audio review.

Before we begin

Guide: Today we're talking about Evaluate Value, Risk, and Harm Together. By the end, you'll know why this matters, how to use it, and what to notice in a real product.

Host: What is the problem we are trying to understand?

Guide: Product cases often quantify intended benefit and describe harm separately in a late review. Evaluate them together because the mechanism creating value may also create risk. Faster matching can reduce effort while increasing reliance on stale data. More verification can improve accuracy while excluding providers with lower capacity.

Guide: Personalisation can improve relevance while collecting sensitive data.

Value and harm are properties of the same system

Host: What changes when we look at value and harm are properties of the same system?

Guide: Map:

Guide: direct users, affected non-users, staff, partners, and communities; intended benefit and how it is produced; burden, lost option, or risk created by the same mechanism; who has power to choose, avoid, detect, contest, and recover; distribution across context and group; evidence, uncertainty, and time horizon.

Guide: Aggregate benefit cannot silently cancel concentrated harm, especially when those harmed have less choice or recourse.

Describe risk as a scenario

Host: What changes when we look at describe risk as a scenario?

Guide: “Privacy risk” or “bias risk” is too abstract to design. Write:

Guide: Because condition or mechanism, affected person may experience event and consequence, while detection/recovery condition makes it more or less severe.

Guide: Assess:

Guide: severity of consequence; likelihood or plausibility with uncertainty; exposure and duration; detectability before harm; reversibility and residual effect; ability and cost to obtain recourse; distribution and structural vulnerability; potential for repetition or amplification.

Guide: Avoid multiplying weak numeric estimates into false precision. Use bands with evidence and rationale. A rare catastrophic or rights-related harm may require a hard constraint even when expected-value arithmetic looks small.

Use a control hierarchy

Host: What changes when we look at use a control hierarchy?

Guide: Prefer controls that reduce risk at its source:

Guide: 1. Avoid: do not create the capability or use case. 2. Reduce scope: limit population, data, authority, attribute, or consequence. 3. Change the system: remove hazardous defaults, repair data/policy, redesign workflow. 4. Prevent: validation, permissions, separation of duties, accessible interaction, safe thresholds. 5.

Guide: Detect and contain: monitoring, audit, anomaly detection, pause, suppression. 6. Recover and remedy: correction, fallback, appeal, support, compensation, communication. 7. Inform: explanation and warning where people can act.

Guide: Disclosure is a weak primary mitigation when the organisation can remove the hazard. Consent does not legitimise an unsafe or coercive arrangement. Human review is not protective without capacity, evidence, authority, and independence.

Include misuse, non-use, and externalities

Host: What changes when we look at include misuse, non-use, and externalities?

Guide: Ask how the system may be used beyond intent, including internal shortcut, coercive use, proxy decisions, data reuse, and optimisation pressure. Examine people who cannot or will not use it: does the alternate route deliver comparable quality or punish refusal?

Guide: Look beyond the transaction. Verification notifications consume attention; data infrastructure requires maintenance; provider ranking changes demand; staff absorb edge cases; environmental and vendor dependencies create longer-term costs. These externalities belong in the product decision.

Evaluate controls as real service capabilities

Host: What changes when we look at evaluate controls as real service capabilities?

Guide: Test controls under the condition that invokes them. Can a screen-reader user perceive conflict? Can support suppress a record at night? Does rollback correct every channel? Can a smaller clinic use the alternate route? Does an appeal change outcome? Monitor the control’s performance and unintended burden.

Guide: Risk is dynamic. Reassess when population, policy, data, model, source, channel, business incentive, or authority changes. A safe pilot boundary does not automatically remain safe at scale.

Transfer example: lending pre-approval

Host: What changes when we look at transfer example: lending pre-approval?

Guide: A fast pre-approval flow creates convenience and commercial value. It can also expose sensitive data, reproduce unequal historical access, appear more final than it is, or deny people without usable recourse.

Guide: Strong mitigation may require narrowing the use, separating education from decision, providing adverse-action explanation and correction, independent review, outcome monitoring, and refusing automation where the institution cannot carry the risk.

Where this can go wrong

Host: What changes when we look at where this can go wrong?

Guide: Benefit ledger only: intended value is quantified while burden and externality remain prose. Average-value cancellation: aggregate improvement hides concentrated severe harm. Risk nouns: “privacy” or “bias” appears without a scenario, mechanism, or affected person. Likelihood theatre: unsupported numbers make uncertain harm look managed.

Guide: Disclosure mitigation: warning transfers a preventable institutional risk to the user. Human-review fiction: oversight lacks capacity, evidence, authority, or independence. Checklist closure: naming a control is treated as evidence it works. Unowned residual risk: no legitimate role accepts, monitors, pauses, and remedies.

The harder decision

Host: Where does the harder design decision appear?

Guide: Senior practitioners connect ethics to product mechanics and decision rights. They challenge who defines acceptable risk, whose evidence is missing, and whether people can realistically refuse or recover.

Guide: They distinguish reversible experimentation from exposure that creates lasting harm and are prepared to recommend narrower scope or no build when the organisation lacks protective capability.

Product example

Host: Can we see that in a concrete product situation?

Guide: Illustrative scenario: a rent-payment product. Automatic payment may reduce missed deadlines while creating overdraft risk for people with irregular income. Evaluate benefit, business value, reversibility, notice, support burden, and unequal harm together. A high adoption rate is not a complete answer if a subgroup bears the cost of the design.

Try it yourself

Host: What should I notice the next time I use a product?

Guide: Choose a product that handles health, money, identity, work, safety, or automated judgement. Find its main value promise, then ask which group could experience the opposite outcome. Is the protection prevention, detection, recovery, or merely disclosure? Try to locate the accountable appeal or remedy.

Guide: If it ends at “contact support,” inspect whether support can actually change the decision.
